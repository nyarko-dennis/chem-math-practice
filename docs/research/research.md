Operational Definition of Terms
Operational definitions explain how key terms and concepts in a study are understood and measured. They help ensure consistency, clarity, and accuracy, allowing readers and researchers to interpret the study variables in the same way.

Examples
Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice of Infection Prevention and Control Among Nursing Students in the Greater Accra Region, Ghana

Knowledge: The level of understanding nursing students have regarding infection prevention practices, including hand hygiene, use of personal protective equipment, and safe disposal of sharps. Measured using a structured questionnaire.

Attitude: The beliefs, feelings, and perceptions of nursing students toward infection prevention, assessed using a Likert-scale survey to determine positivity or negativity toward adherence.

Practice: The actual behaviors and adherence of nursing students to infection prevention protocols, measured through observation or self-reported checklists.

Infection Prevention: The strategies implemented to minimize the spread of infections in healthcare settings, including hand hygiene, proper use of personal protective equipment, and safe disposal of sharps.

Note: Operational definitions make abstract concepts measurable, ensuring that research data is collected, analyzed, and interpreted consistently.

How to Write Study Objectives
Start with the Main Objective: clearly state the broad purpose of the study in one sentence. It should directly connect to the problem statement.

Break into Specific Objectives: write 3–5 clear, concise objectives that:

Cover each key aspect of the study;

Correspond to each specific research question;

Are achievable and measurable.

Be consistent; avoid overlapping objectives or objectives outside the scope of your study.

Use action-oriented language: (to assess, to describe, to compare, to evaluate, to explore).

Ensure alignment; the objectives should match the background, problem statement, and methodology.

Characteristics of Good Objectives (SMART); They should be:
Specific (clear and precise),

Measurable (able to be assessed),

Achievable (realistic within scope),

Relevant (directly linked to the study problem),

Time-bound (if applicable, with a defined timeframe).

Study Area, Study Site & Study Setting
Study Area
Refers to the broader geographic, administrative, or ecological region where the study is conducted.

Represents the setting or domain from which study participants are drawn (e.g., a district, city, or region).

Provides contextual background such as population size, demographics, infrastructure, and environment.

Helps define the sampling frame and affects external validity (generalizability of findings).

Some writers include a location or area map to visually show where the study area is situated.

For community-based studies, include:

Major occupations and way of life of residents.

Presence of schools, hospitals, markets, or significant landmarks.

Key socio-economic or cultural features relevant to the study.

Study Area, Study Site & Study Setting Cont'd
Study Site
The specific location(s) within the study area where data collection or observation occurs.

Examples: hospital ward, clinic, school, community center, or health post.

This is where the operational work happens, recruitment, interviews, measurements, and sampling.

Ensures internal validity by maintaining control, consistency, and minimizing bias.

The departments or units involved.

Services provided and how they relate to the study.

The Implementation Gap (Top Block)
The implementation gap is the disconnect between what research discovers and what is actually practiced in policy, healthcare, education, or community settings.

In other words, it's when evidence exists, but action doesn't follow.

Example: Despite research showing hand hygiene reduces infections, compliance among health workers remains low.

Why It Matters
Limits the impact of research on improving lives.

Wastes resources spent on producing unused evidence.

Slows innovation and policy reform.

Data Collection Procedure
The data collection procedure explains the systematic steps and processes undertaken by the researcher to gather data from respondents using the chosen data collection tools.

It outlines how, where, and when data were collected, ensuring the process is transparent, ethical, and replicable.

Description in Academic Research: In academic research, this section provides a comprehensive account of how data were gathered and the measures taken to ensure quality, accuracy, and ethical compliance.

Typical Components Include:
Introductory Letter and Permissions: An introductory letter from the researcher’s department or institution is submitted to obtain permission from relevant authorities. For facility-based studies, it is sent to the hospital or institutional administration. For community-based studies, it is submitted to local or community leaders for approval to conduct the study.

Ethical Considerations:
All ethical standards and protocols were strictly observed.

Approval and authorization were obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB), facility ethics committee, or community authorities before data collection began.

Participants’ rights to confidentiality, anonymity, and voluntary participation were ensured throughout the process.

Sample: Data Collection Procedure
The data collection procedure for this study involved administering a structured questionnaire to patients attending the Orthopedic Clinic at the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital during the study period. Prior to data collection, an introductory letter from the Department of Nursing, Central University, was submitted to the hospital administration to obtain permission to conduct the study. Ethical approval was also sought, and all relevant ethical considerations, such as confidentiality, voluntary participation, and informed consent, were strictly observed.

A convenience sampling technique was employed to recruit eligible participants who met the inclusion criteria. Patients who had completed their consultation or treatment sessions were approached, and the purpose of the study was clearly explained to them. Those who voluntarily agreed to participate signed a written consent form before data collection commenced. The questionnaire was self-administered and designed to collect quantitative data relevant to the study objectives. Participants were guided on how to complete the questionnaire, and assistance was provided when necessary to ensure clarity and accuracy. Completed questionnaires were collected immediately after completion to maintain a high response rate.

Where necessary, research assistants were engaged and trained on the objectives of the study, ethical principles, and procedures for questionnaire administration to ensure uniformity and reliability during data collection. The entire process was supervised by the researcher to ensure completeness and consistency of responses. Data collected were checked for accuracy, coded, and prepared for statistical analysis. Throughout the process, participants’ privacy and confidentiality were maintained, and data were stored securely. The study aimed to collect reliable quantitative data on patients’ waiting times, satisfaction levels, and their interrelationship, providing insight into the quality of service delivery at the Orthopedic Clinic.

Research Design (Sample Description - Quantitative Study)
This study adopted a descriptive cross-sectional design using a quantitative approach to data collection to assess job satisfaction among nurses in small rural hospitals. In a descriptive cross-sectional design, researchers collect data at a single point in time to describe characteristics of a population and measure prevalence of outcomes, such as job satisfaction and its associated factors among nurses, without manipulating variables. A quantitative approach systematically gathers numerical data through structured tools like surveys and questionnaires and applies statistical analysis to identify patterns and predictors of satisfaction, burnout, or intent to leave. This design is suitable for the study because it efficiently provides measurable, generalizable insights on current nursing workforce issues and informs workforce planning and policy.

Research Design (Sample Description - Qualitative Study)
This study adopted a descriptive phenomenological design using a qualitative approach to data collection to explore coping strategies among oncology nurses caring for terminally ill patients. A descriptive phenomenological design focuses on understanding and describing individuals’ lived experiences regarding a phenomenon, capturing the essence of their perceptions, emotions, and coping mechanisms without manipulating the environment. A qualitative approach collects non-numerical data through methods such as in-depth interviews, focus group discussions, and observations, allowing rich, detailed, and context-specific insights into participants’ experiences. This design is suitable because it provides deep understanding of the “how” and “why” of nurses’ coping strategies, informing interventions, support programs, and policy decisions in palliative care nursing.

Discussion Section in Research
The Discussion is the part of a research paper that comes after the Introduction, Methods, and Results sections.

It is where you interpret, analyse, and explain your findings rather than simply presenting them.

The purpose of the Discussion is to answer the question: “So what?” showing why your results matter, how they address your research questions, and how they relate to existing studies and theories.

In essence, the Discussion moves the paper from “Here are the results” to “Here is what the results mean.”

Functions of the Discussion Section
Interpretation: Explains the meaning and significance of your findings.

Comparison: Relates your results to those of previous studies, showing where they support or contradict existing knowledge.

Theoretical linkage: Connects your findings to theoretical frameworks or concepts that guided your study.

Limitations: Acknowledges the boundaries, challenges, or weaknesses of your study.

Implications: Discusses how your findings contribute to theory, policy, or practice and suggests directions for future research.

Validity and Reliability of Data Collection Instruments
Validity
Validity refers to the extent to which an instrument measures what it is intended to measure.

It determines the accuracy, appropriateness, and truthfulness of the data collected.

Types of Validity:
Content Validity: Ensures the instrument covers all relevant areas of the concept. Example: A questionnaire on antenatal care must include items on both knowledge and practices.

Construct Validity: Ensures the instrument truly reflects the theoretical concept. Example: A stress scale should measure psychological stress, not physical fatigue.

Criterion Validity: Compares results with an external standard or existing validated tool. Example: Comparing a new depression scale with the Beck Depression Inventory.

How to Achieve Validity
Conduct extensive literature review before designing the instrument.

Align items directly with study objectives and conceptual framework.

Seek expert review from supervisors or subject specialists.

Pilot test (pre-test) the instrument with a small group to identify unclear or irrelevant items.

Conceptual Framework in Research
A conceptual framework is a visual or written representation of the key concepts, variables, or constructs in a study and the assumed relationships among them. It serves as a blueprint for your research, guiding data collection, analysis, and interpretation.

It is usually developed from literature review, existing theories, and research questions.

Helps clarify what you will study and how variables relate.

Steps to Develop a Conceptual Framework
Identify key concepts/variables

Independent variables (what influences outcomes)

Dependent variables (outcomes or effects)

Intervening/moderating variables (factors that may affect the relationship)

Review literature and theories: Find previous studies or theories supporting your variables.

Define relationships: Explain how independent variables influence dependent variables.

Visualize the framework: Use boxes/circles for variables and arrows to show relationships.

Justify the framework: Explain why these variables and relationships are important.

Theoretical vs Conceptual Frameworks
Theoretical Framework
Acts as the blueprint or guide for research (Grant & Osanloo, 2014). It helps the researcher structure the study based on established knowledge.

Provides the foundation upon which the research is built, ensuring it is grounded in theory.

Functions like a map or travel plan (Sinclair, 2007), guiding the researcher on what to focus on.

Based on existing theory or theories, e.g., motivation theory, health belief model.

Explains why phenomena occur and predicts relationships between variables.

Conceptual Framework
Developed by the researcher using insights from the theoretical framework.

Illustrates key concepts, variables, and their relationships in the study.

Shows the pathway of the research, helping to clarify how the study will answer its research questions.

Provides a practical structure for data collection, analysis, and interpretation.

Key Points
Both frameworks ground the research in theory, giving it credibility and rigor.

Make research findings more meaningful, understandable, and generalizable.

Theoretical framework answers the “why”, conceptual framework answers the “how” of the study.

Numeric response formats that require respondents to provide numerical data (e.g., age, years of experience, frequency).

Ethical Considerations in Research Methodology
Ethical considerations refer to the principles, standards, and procedures that guide researchers to conduct studies in a morally acceptable, respectful, and responsible manner, ensuring the rights, safety, and dignity of participants are protected throughout the research process.

Purpose
To ensure research is conducted with integrity, fairness, and accountability.

To protect participants from harm, deception, or exploitation.

To enhance the credibility and acceptability of the study's findings.

Steps in Ethical Consideration Process
1. Introductory Letter:
Obtain an introductory letter from your school or department. Submit it to the institution or organization where the study will be conducted.

2. Ethical Approval:
If the study is within a health institution, submit the proposal to the Institutional Review Board (IRB) or Ethics Committee for ethical clearance. If it's a community-based study, obtain permission from local or community authorities before data collection.

3. Informed Consent:
Explain the purpose, benefits, risks, and rights of participants clearly. Participation must be voluntary, and participants should be free to withdraw at any time. Obtain written or verbal consent (depending on literacy level and context).

4. Confidentiality and Anonymity:
Protect participants' identities by removing names or personal identifiers. Store data securely (e.g., password-protected devices, locked files).

How to Write the Summary
Paragraph 1: Background & Problem + Aim/Objectives
Begin with your research background: a brief context of why the study matters.

Then state your problem statement: what gap or issue you addressed.

End the paragraph by emphasizing the aim or specific objectives of your study.

Example: "This study aimed to..." or "The objectives were..."

Paragraph 2: Methods Overview
Summarize your methodological approach:

Research design (e.g., cross-sectional survey, case study)

Study setting (where or in what context)

Target population and sample size

Data collection tools (questionnaire, interviews, observations)

Data analysis approach (statistical tests, thematic analysis)

Keep this concise but clear so the reader knows how you conducted your study.

How to Write the Summary Cont'd
Paragraph 3: Key Results
Highlight the most significant findings of your study (not every detail, but the major outcomes).

Phrase them in a way that links to your objectives: e.g., "Objective one found that...", "A major correlation was observed between..."

Paragraph 4: Conclusion & Recommendations
Conclude with the main takeaway: what do the results mean in the context of the research problem.

Provide brief recommendations: what action, policy, or future research you suggest based on your findings.

Key Points
Keep the summary clear, concise and readable.

Avoid overly technical detail or long tables.

Make sure the structure reflects your entire research flow: background → problem → aim/objectives → methods → results → conclusions/recommendations.

Write the summary when you have completed your project, so you have the full picture.

Tailor it to your institution's word-limit and style guidelines.

Designing Data Collection Tools for Retrospective and Prospective Studies
2. Prospective Studies
In prospective studies, data are collected directly from participants as events unfold or during the study period.

The data collection tool may include questionnaires, interview guides, or observation checklists, depending on the research design (quantitative or qualitative).

The instrument is developed in alignment with the specific objectives and is usually pre-tested or piloted before data collection begins.

Sections of the tool often include:
Section A: Demographic information

Section B–D: Main variables based on study objectives

Ethical considerations such as informed consent and confidentiality are strictly observed during data collection.

Example:
A structured questionnaire was designed to collect data from pregnant women attending antenatal clinics to assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding nutrition.

Using Standardized and Validated Tools in Questionnaire Design
In quantitative research, researchers sometimes use standardized or validated instruments when designing their data collection tools.

A standardized tool is a questionnaire or scale that has been previously developed, tested, and validated for reliability and accuracy in measuring a specific concept or phenomenon.

Application in Research:
Researchers may adopt or adapt an existing validated tool to suit the context of their own study.

For instance, when studying stress levels, a researcher may use an established tool such as the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS).

If the standardized tool contains items that are not directly relevant, the researcher can select or modify only the items that align with the study's objectives or population.

This process ensures that the data collection instrument remains scientifically grounded while being contextually appropriate.

Relevance and Importance:
Enhances validity and reliability of the study by using proven measures.

Promotes comparability of findings with other similar studies.

Saves time and resources since the instrument has been pre-tested and validated.

Increases the credibility and acceptance of research results among scholars and practitioners.

Example:
A researcher assessing nurses' stress levels adopted selected items from the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) to suit the hospital work environment while maintaining the validity of the original instrument.

Qualitative Data Analysis
Qualitative analysis focuses on non-numerical data such as interview responses, narratives, or focus group discussions.

Key Processes in Qualitative Analysis:
1. Transcription:
This involves converting audio-recorded interviews or discussions into written text verbatim (word-for-word).

Transcription is usually done immediately after each interview to ensure accuracy.

It provides the textual data needed for analysis.

2. Familiarization:
The researcher reads and re-reads the transcripts to understand emerging patterns and meanings.

3. Coding:
Segments of text that relate to similar ideas are labeled with codes (e.g., "stress factors," "coping strategies").

4. Categorization and Thematic Analysis:
Related codes are grouped into themes and subthemes that reflect the core issues.

Example: From codes like "lack of PPE" and "workload stress," a theme may emerge as "Occupational Challenges."

5. Thematic Content Analysis:
Thematic Content Analysis is the most common method, involving identifying, analyzing, and reporting patterns (themes) within the data.

6. Interpretation:
Themes are interpreted to explain participants' perspectives in relation to the study objectives and existing literature.

RESEARCH
Health system research entails systematic collection or analysis of data with the intent to develop generalizable knowledge to understand health challenges, often aiming to find solutions or improve health outcomes for populations beyond the specific individuals or settings studied. Key goals include testing hypotheses, establishing formal protocols, and ensuring findings can be applied broadly to a larger population (World Health Organization, 2025).

According to the Oxford Learner's Dictionaries, research is a careful study of a subject, particularly to discover new facts or information.

Merriam-Webster's dictionary also defines research primarily as the act of investigating or studying a subject closely or the systematic process of scientific inquiry. Both definitions emphasize the systematic pursuit of knowledge and understanding.

Research=re+search i.e search again
Research Methods & Methodology
Research methods encompass the systematic approaches and techniques employed to collect, analyse, and interpret data in order to answer research questions. They include various strategies such as surveys, experiments, case studies, and observational studies, each selected based on the research objectives, questions, and context. These methods provide the framework for gathering empirical evidence and ensuring the validity and reliability of research findings.

Research Methodology: This refers to the set of systematic principles, procedures, and practices that guide how a study is designed, executed, and reported, ensuring that the findings are valid, reliable, credible, and useful for both theory and practice. It emphasizes decisions related to topic framing, sampling, measurement, ethics, data collection, data analysis, and reporting (Aguinis, 2023).

Background of the Study
The Background of the Study provides the reader with the context and history needed to understand the research issue. It explains what is known already, the developments in the field, and sets the stage for identifying what is missing.

It answers questions such as: What problem is being studied? Why is it important? What has previous research shown and where are conflicting findings?

The Background establishes relevance: it should give enough data, statistics or literature to show that the problem matters (e.g., disease burden, social impact).

It usually ends by narrowing down to what is not known, the research gap, which naturally leads into the statement of the problem.

How to Write Background of the Study
Begin broadly: introduce the public health or clinical issue, show its global and local significance, then narrow to the specific aspect your study addresses.

Provide relevant literature: review key studies that relate to your topic. Highlight what was found, what is consistent, and where contradictions exist.

Link to your objectives: each specific objective should have a short background explanation with supporting data or statistics (e.g., knowledge gaps, disease prevalence, attitude patterns, barriers to care).

Show the research gap: after summarizing existing knowledge, clearly state what is missing (populations not studied, contexts lacking, or methods not applied).

Explain relevance / justification: show why filling the gap matters for practice, policy, or knowledge building. Use numbers and evidence to strengthen your point.

End strongly: the background should conclude by highlighting the main topic, title, or overall aim of your study, so the reader sees how the study naturally emerges from the context provided.

Research Problem vs Knowledge Gap: Defining the Issue, Justifying the Study
Research Problem: The specific issue, difficulty, or situation that the researcher wants to investigate.

Rooted in real-world practice (something happening that needs attention) or theory (something unclear or debated).

Forms the foundation of the study, it tells us what the problem is.

Example: Nurses in psychiatric departments often lack adequate knowledge and application of legal and ethical responsibilities, which exposes both nurses and patients to risks.

Knowledge Gap: The missing information, limited evidence, or unanswered questions in the existing body of research.

Rooted in literature review: what previous studies have not yet covered.

Role: Justifies the study; it tells us what is not yet known about the problem.

Example (Health): Few studies have assessed the legal and ethical knowledge of psychiatric nurses in Egypt, leaving limited evidence on how well they apply these responsibilities in practice.

A strong problem statement is built by combining:
The Research Problem: What issue exists?

The Knowledge Gap: What is not yet known about it?

Structure

A problem tree helps identify a research problem by showing its causes and effects, clarifying the issue, focusing the study, and justifying why it needs research.

Types of Research by Data Source
1. Primary Research:
Researcher collects new, original data directly from participants or experiments. It provides firsthand evidence and allows more control over data quality.

Methods: surveys, laboratory tests, clinical trials, observations, interviews.

Examples: conducting a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to test a new malaria treatment; interviewing pregnant women to assess knowledge of Rhesus incompatibility.

2. Secondary Research:
Researcher analyzes existing data collected by others. It is cost-effective and useful for summarizing large bodies of evidence, though quality depends on the original studies.

Methods: systematic reviews (a structured process of collecting and analyzing all published studies on a topic to answer a specific question) and meta-analysis (a statistical method of combining results from multiple studies to get a stronger, more reliable conclusion), literature reviews, national health database analysis.

Examples: systematic review of 20 studies on hypertension management in Africa; meta-analysis of clinical trials on diabetes medications; analyzing Ghana Health Service records on maternal mortality trends.

Research Topic/Title
A research topic is the specific subject or problem a researcher chooses to study. It identifies what the study is about and sets the focus for the research.

A good research topic is clear, relevant, researchable, and significant. It is usually formulated by:

Identifying a problem or gap in knowledge.

Reviewing literature to see what has been studied.

Narrowing the subject to a manageable scope.

Stating it in a clear, concise way (e.g., "Knowledge and Attitudes of Nursing Students toward Infection Prevention").

Most research topics contain or are formed by two variables.

Variables are the key factors or characteristics that are measured, observed, or controlled in a study.

Example: The Effect of Health Education (IV) on Hand Hygiene Practices (DV) among Nursing Students.

The Independent Variable (IV) is the factor that is manipulated or assumed to influence the outcome (e.g., knowledge of infection prevention), while the Dependent Variable (DV) is the outcome or effect being studied, which depends on the IV (e.g., practice of infection prevention).

Qualitative/Exploratory Research
A type of research that seeks to understand how people think, feel, and experience certain situations. It is used when the aim is to explore meanings, behaviors, social interactions, and cultural contexts rather than measure numbers. Explores meanings, experiences, beliefs, and human behavior in depth.

Data: Words, observations, interviews, focus groups, documents, and open-ended responses.

Outcome: Provides rich, detailed descriptions and insights that help explain "why" and "how" something happens.

Subtypes:
Case Study: intensive study of a single person, group, event, or institution.

Phenomenology: explores lived experiences of individuals (e.g., patient's experience with chronic pain).

Ethnography: studies culture or community by immersing in their daily life.

Grounded Theory: builds new theories directly from collected data.

Narrative Research: collects and interprets personal life stories.

Action Research: research conducted by practitioners to improve their own practice or solve a problem.

Quantitative Research
A type of research that focuses on collecting and analyzing numerical data to measure variables, identify patterns, test hypotheses, and determine cause-and-effect relationships. Measurement, prediction, and statistical analysis.

Data: Numbers, surveys, experiments, tests, and structured questionnaires.

Outcome: Produces statistical results that can be generalized to larger populations.

Subtypes:
Descriptive: describes what exists, such as characteristics or frequencies (e.g., rate of malaria in a community).

Correlational: examines whether two variables are related (e.g., stress and academic performance).

Experimental: tests cause-and-effect using control and random assignment.

Quasi-Experimental: similar to experimental but lacks full control (e.g., no randomization).

Causal-Comparative (Ex Post Facto): compares groups after the fact to identify possible causes.

Sampling Procedure
The sampling procedure refers to the process and techniques used by a researcher to select a portion (sample) of a population from which data are collected for the study.

Sampling in research methodology means selecting a representative subset of the population to make generalizations about the whole population.

Sampling Procedure Cont'd: Types of Sampling
1. Probability Sampling
Every member of the population has a known and equal chance of being selected. Ensures objectivity, representativeness, and allows generalization of findings to the entire population.

Subtypes:
Simple Random Sampling: Every individual has an equal chance (e.g., random number selection). Used when the population list is available.

Systematic Sampling: Every k-th individual on a list is selected after a random start. Used when population members are ordered.

Stratified Sampling: Population is divided into subgroups (strata) such as gender or age, and samples are drawn from each. Ensures representation across key subgroups.

Cluster Sampling: Population is divided into clusters (e.g., schools, hospitals), and a few clusters are randomly selected for study. Used for large or geographically dispersed populations.

Significance
Produces highly reliable and generalizable results.

Reduces selection bias.

Ideal for quantitative research where statistical inference is needed.

Limitations
Requires a complete sampling frame.

Can be costly and time-consuming.

Reliability in Qualitative Research
Reliability in qualitative research is not about replicating results exactly, but about ensuring that the process of data collection and analysis is consistent, stable, and dependable. It focuses on whether another researcher following the same procedures could reach similar interpretations.

To ensure reliability, researchers use strategies such as:
Audit Trail: Keeping clear and detailed documentation of every step of the research process, including data collection, coding decisions, and analytical procedures, so that others can follow the process.

Dependability Audit: Having an external reviewer assess the research process to verify consistency and logical progression.

Code-Recode Strategy: Coding the same data twice at different times and comparing results to check consistency in interpretation.

Inter-coder Agreement: When multiple researchers analyze the same data, they compare their codes and resolve differences through discussion to ensure dependability.

Trustworthiness in Qualitative Research
To replace traditional notions of validity and reliability, Lincoln and Guba (1985) proposed four major criteria for establishing trustworthiness in qualitative studies:

Credibility: This corresponds to internal validity in quantitative research. It is about ensuring confidence in the truth of the findings. Credibility is enhanced through triangulation, member checking, prolonged engagement, and peer debriefing.

Dependability: This corresponds to reliability in quantitative research. It refers to the stability and consistency of data over time. Dependability is achieved through maintaining an audit trail and using techniques such as code-recode procedures.

Confirmability: This is the qualitative equivalent of objectivity. It ensures that the findings are shaped by the participants' voices and not by the researcher's bias, motivation, or interest. Confirmability can be achieved through reflexive journaling and maintaining detailed documentation of analytical decisions.

Transferability: This corresponds to external validity or generalizability. It focuses on how well the study's findings can be applied to other contexts or settings. Transferability is supported by providing a rich, thick description of the context so that readers can determine whether the findings are applicable elsewhere.

Theoretical Framework
A theoretical framework is a structure that guides your research by relying on a theory or set of concepts to explain, predict, or understand phenomena. It connects your study to existing knowledge and provides a lens through which you interpret your findings.

It helps define key variables and the relationships between them.

It provides a rationale for your research questions, objectives, and methodology.

It is usually grounded in established theories or conceptual models.

Example: If you are studying "the effect of nurse-patient communication on patient satisfaction," your theoretical framework might be Peplau's Interpersonal Relations Theory, which explains how nurse-patient interactions influence patient outcomes.

Steps to Develop a Theoretical Framework in Research
Identify your research problem/topic: Clearly define what you are studying.

Review relevant literature and theories: Find theories or models that relate to your topic. Example: Health Belief Model, Social Cognitive Theory.

Select an appropriate theory or model: Choose the one that best explains the relationships between your variables.

Define key concepts and variables: Independent variables (factors influencing outcome) and Dependent variables (outcomes you measure).

Specify relationships: Show how the theory explains the connection between variables. Often illustrated in a diagram.

Justify the framework: Explain why this theory is suitable for your study.

Commonly used Theories in Health, Nursing, and Social science Research
Health Belief Model (HBM): predicts health behaviors based on perceptions.

Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB): explains intentions that lead to behaviors.

Social Cognitive Theory (SCT): learning through observation and social interaction.

Stress and Coping Theory (Lazarus & Folkman): how individuals manage stress.

Self-Efficacy Theory (Bandura): belief in one's ability affects behavior.

Social Support Theory: social networks influence health outcomes.

Diffusion of Innovations Theory: adoption of new ideas or technology.

Ecological / Socio-Ecological Model: health is influenced by multiple levels of factors.

Roy's Adaptation Model: patients adapt to environmental changes.

Florence Nightingale's Environmental Theory: health influenced by environment.

Virginia Henderson's Need Theory: assisting patients to meet 14 basic needs.

Hildegard Peplau's Interpersonal Relations Theory: nurse-patient relationship and communication.

Dorothea Orem's Self-Care Deficit Nursing Theory: nurses assist patients with self-care deficits.

Jean Watson's Theory of Human Caring: caring as central to nursing practice.

Madeleine Leininger's Culture Care Theory: culture influences health and care.

Betty Neuman's Systems Model: patient as holistic system responding to stressors.

Patricia Benner's Novice to Expert Theory: nursing competence develops over time.

Pender's Health Promotion Model: focuses on factors influencing health-promoting behaviors.

Validity and Reliability of Data Collection Instruments
Reliability
Reliability refers to the consistency, stability, and dependability of an instrument in measuring a concept over time.

A reliable tool produces similar results when used repeatedly under similar conditions.

Types of Reliability:
Test-Retest Reliability: Administering the same instrument to the same group at two different times to check consistency.

Inter-Rater Reliability: Ensures consistency among different observers or raters.

Example: Two nurses independently recording patient pain levels.

Internal Consistency Reliability: Checks the consistency of responses across items within the same test (e.g., Cronbach's Alpha).

How to Achieve Reliability:
Use clear, unambiguous, and standardized questions.

Provide training for research assistants to ensure uniform administration.

Pre-test the instrument to identify and correct inconsistencies.

Use Cronbach’s Alpha (≥0.7) to test internal consistency for quantitative tools.

Validity and Reliability in Qualitative Research
In qualitative research, validity and reliability are understood in terms of trustworthiness rather than numerical accuracy. This is because qualitative studies explore meanings, experiences, and social contexts rather than measurable variables. Researchers therefore focus on ensuring that their findings are credible, dependable, confirmable, and transferable, all of which together make the study trustworthy.

Validity in Qualitative Research
Validity in qualitative research refers to the accuracy, truthfulness, and authenticity of the findings. It answers the question: Do the findings genuinely represent the participants' experiences and realities?

To ensure validity, researchers often employ several strategies:
Triangulation: This involves using multiple data sources, research methods, or investigators to cross-check and confirm findings. For example, combining interviews, observations, and document analysis strengthens the validity of results.

Member Checking: Participants are asked to review transcripts, interpretations, or conclusions to confirm that their views have been accurately captured.

Rich, Thick Description: Providing detailed and vivid descriptions of participants, settings, and contexts allows readers to fully understand and relate to the findings.

Peer Debriefing: Engaging colleagues or experts to critically review and discuss interpretations helps identify potential researcher bias and enhances objectivity.

Reflexivity: The researcher maintains continuous self-awareness of their assumptions, background, and potential influence on the research process and outcomes.

Recommendations
Recommendations are action-oriented suggestions derived from your research findings.

They indicate what should be done, by whom, and how, based on the evidence your study produced.

Recommendations go beyond simply describing findings; they translate results into practical or policy actions, improvements, or future directions.

They serve as the bridge between "what we found" (results) and "what should happen next" (action).

Characteristics of Strong Recommendations
Specific: Clearly state what action is needed.

Realistic: Feasible within the context of your study and resources.

Actionable: Provide clear steps or strategies for implementation.

Linked to findings: Directly relate to your study objectives, results, and conclusions, maintaining a logical flow.

Recommendations: Purpose and Importance
Recommendations demonstrate the value and practical relevance of your study by showing how findings can lead to change or improvement.

They guide key stakeholders, practitioners, policymakers, institutions, and researchers on how to apply your results to real-world problems.

They show that your research is not purely theoretical but contributes to policy, practice, or knowledge advancement.

Well-written recommendations increase the impact of your research, making it more meaningful and actionable.

They help future researchers identify gaps that remain, propose new directions, and build on your work.

NB: A strong recommendation section turns your research from a completed study into a tool for decision-making and progress, showing exactly how your findings can make a difference.

Subtypes of Qualitative Research Design
1. Case Study: An intensive exploration of a single case, such as an individual, group, institution, or event, to gain deep understanding within its real-life context.

Example: Examining how a rural clinic manages maternal health services despite limited resources.

2. Phenomenology: Focuses on exploring and describing the lived experiences of individuals regarding a specific phenomenon, aiming to understand the essence of their experiences.

Example: Investigating the experiences of patients living with chronic pain to understand their coping mechanisms.

3. Ethnography: Involves studying the culture, customs, and social interactions of a group or community by immersing in their daily life for an extended period.

Example: Observing and interacting with traditional birth attendants in a rural community to understand cultural childbirth practices.

4. Grounded Theory: A systematic approach to data collection and analysis aimed at developing a new theory grounded in the data itself rather than testing existing ones.

Example: Developing a theory on how nurses adapt to work stress based on interview data from healthcare workers.

5. Narrative Research: Centers on personal life stories and how individuals construct meaning from their experiences over time.

Example: Analyzing the life stories of HIV-positive mothers to understand their journey of resilience and stigma.

6. Action Research: Conducted by practitioners (such as teachers, nurses, or managers) to investigate and improve their own practice or solve specific problems in their work environment.

Example: A nursing team implementing and evaluating a new patient-care communication strategy to improve satisfaction.

Mixed Methods Research Design
Mixed Methods Research Design refers to a systematic approach that combines both quantitative and qualitative research methods within a single study to provide a comprehensive understanding of a research problem.

It integrates numerical data (quantitative) with descriptive, experiential, or narrative data (qualitative), allowing the researcher to explore not only what is happening but also why and how it happens. This design recognizes that complex health and social issues are best understood through the integration of both forms of evidence.

Purpose
Integrates numerical trends and personal experiences.

Allows validation of findings through multiple sources.

Data Type
Quantitative Data: Numbers, statistics, surveys, and experiments that measure variables objectively.

Qualitative Data: Words, observations, interviews, focus groups, and open-ended responses that explore meaning and context.

Both types are collected either concurrently (at the same time) or sequentially (one after the other) depending on the research design.

Differences: Proposal Abstract vs Thesis/Actual Research Abstract
Proposal Abstract
Written before the full study is conducted; describes planned research, objectives, methods, and expected outcomes. (students.wlu.ca)

Uses future or tentative tense (e.g., "will examine", "is planned to") since results are not yet available.

Emphasises rationale, research gap, and study design.

Actual Project / Research Thesis Abstract
Written after the study is completed; summarises the problem addressed, methodology, findings, conclusions, and significance. (libguides.usc.edu)

Uses past tense (e.g., "was conducted", "found that") because results are available.

Focuses on what was done and what was found, rather than what will be done.

Types of Research by Design
Observational Studies: researcher observes without intervening. Examples include:
Cross-sectional

Cohort Research

Case-Control Research

Ecological Research

Cross-sectional: collects data at a single point in time to measure variables and describe relationships. It is like taking a “snapshot” of a population. Example, assessing the prevalence of hypertension among nurses at a teaching hospital during routine screening.

Cohort Research: follows a group of people who share common characteristics or exposure to see how it affects outcomes over time. Can be prospective or retrospective. Example: following two groups of smokers and non-smokers among health workers to observe the incidence of respiratory illnesses.

Case-Control Research: compares people with a specific condition (cases) to those without it (controls) to identify risk factors. It is useful for rare conditions. Example: comparing women with pre-eclampsia (cases) to women without it (controls) to identify risk factors such as age or parity.

Ecological Research: studies groups or populations rather than individuals, often using existing data sources. It examines associations at the community or population level. Example: comparing regions with high versus low vaccination rates to assess differences in measles outbreaks.

Types of Research by Design Cont'd
Experimental or Interventional Research: involves testing interventions under controlled conditions. Example: a randomized controlled trial to test a new vaccine for malaria.

Quasi-Experimental Research: similar to experimental but without full randomization. Example: evaluating patient recovery outcomes before and after implementing a new post-surgery care protocol in a hospital ward.

Forming Themes in a Literature Review
1. Start with the Research Focus
Themes should be linked to your specific objectives or research questions.

2. Use Discipline and Journal Guidelines

3. Derive from Patterns in the Literature
Group studies that share common findings, methods, or theories.

Identify contrasting views and build sub-themes if necessary.

4. Ensure Logical Flow
Move from broad to specific themes.

5. Keep It Critical, Not Just Descriptive

Hierarchy of Scientific Evidence
The hierarchy of scientific evidence ranks research studies based on the strength, reliability, and quality of their findings. Higher levels represent research designs that minimize bias and provide more trustworthy evidence for decision-making.

Levels of Evidence (from Strongest to Weakest)
Systematic Reviews & Meta-Analyses

Combine data from multiple studies to produce comprehensive conclusions.

Provide the highest level of evidence.

Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs)

Participants are randomly assigned to intervention or control groups.

Considered the gold standard for testing cause-and-effect relationships.

Cohort Studies

Follow groups (cohorts) over time to observe outcomes based on exposures.

Provide prospective observational evidence.

Hierarchy of Scientific Evidence Cont'd
Case-Control Studies

Compare individuals with a condition (cases) to those without (controls).

Useful for studying rare diseases or outcomes.

Cross-Sectional Studies

Collect data at a single point in time.

Describe prevalence and associations but not causality.

Case Reports and Case Series

Describe observations of individual or small groups of patients.

Provide preliminary evidence or highlight new phenomena.

Expert Opinions, Narrative Reviews, and Editorials

Based on professional judgment rather than empirical data.

Lowest level of scientific evidence.

Sample Size Determination in Qualitative Research (Bottom Panel)
Sample size determination in qualitative studies focuses on depth of understanding rather than numerical representation. Unlike quantitative research, which uses statistical formulas (like Cochran’s or Yamane’s), qualitative research aims to explore meanings, experiences, or perceptions in detail.

Key Characteristics
Non-statistical approach: The emphasis is on information richness, not sample quantity.

Flexible and iterative: The sample size may evolve as data collection proceeds.

Purposeful selection: Participants are chosen based on their ability to provide relevant and in-depth information about the phenomenon under study.

Typical Sample Sizes
Small and manageable, often 10–30 participants depending on the study design. May vary by qualitative method:

Phenomenology: 6–10 participants

Case study: 4–10 participants

Grounded theory: 20–30 participants

Focus group: 6–12 participants per group

Difference from Quantitative Studies
Quantitative: Seeks generalization; sample size determined statistically.

Qualitative: Seeks understanding; sample size determined conceptually through data saturation.

How to Write the Conclusion: Start + Points to Consider
How to start writing the conclusion
Begin by restating your research aim/question(s) in different words (don’t copy-paste exactly from the introduction).

Then provide a brief overview of what you found and how you found it (without repeating every detail).

Highlight your contribution: what gap did you fill, how does your study add new knowledge.

Points to consider / Key elements
Clear answer to your main research question(s).

Broad summary of key findings and how they link to what you set out to do.

Discussion of implications: for theory, practice, policy, or future research.

Closing remarks: something that leaves the reader with a final thought, call to action, or connection back to the beginning.

Acknowledgement of the limitations of your study (briefly).

Recommendations and/or suggestions for future research or action.

What to avoid
Don’t introduce new data or new major arguments in the conclusion.

Using vague phrases (“In conclusion...”, “To summarize...”) without depth.

Don’t simply repeat large chunks of your discussion or results sections. The conclusion should synthesize, not regurgitate.

Types of Variables in Research
Assessing the Impact of Evidence-Based Practice Training on Nursing Students' Knowledge and Skills at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital

Independent Variable: The main factor you manipulate or observe to see its effect. Example: Training on evidence-based practice.

Dependent Variable: The outcome or result that changes because of the independent variable. Example: Level of EBP knowledge or skill among nurses.

Mediating Variable: Explains how or why the independent variable influences the dependent variable. Example: Increased motivation or access to learning materials.

Moderating Variable: Affects the strength or direction of the relationship between the independent and dependent variables. Example: Years of work experience or educational level.

Confounding Variable: A hidden factor related to both the independent and dependent variables that can distort the results. Example: Previous EBP exposure or institutional policies.

Control Variable: A variable you keep constant to make sure changes are only due to your independent variable. Example: Age, gender, or clinical department of participants.

Conceptual Frameworks in Research Cont'd
A conceptual framework illustrates the key concepts and variables of a study and how they relate.

It identifies independent, dependent, and, where applicable, mediating or moderating variables.

It is grounded in existing theory or literature that supports the proposed relationships.

It provides a visual or narrative model to guide research design, data collection, and analysis.

It helps to clarify the research problem, objectives, and underlying assumptions of the study.

It ensures coherence and validity by aligning the framework with the methodology and interpretation of findings.

It can be presented as a diagram showing arrows or links between variables to illustrate cause-and-effect relationships.

It concludes with a narrative explanation describing how variables were chosen, their relationships, and how they will be measured.
