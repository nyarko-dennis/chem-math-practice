# Pain Management & the WHO Analgesic Ladder

## Why Treating Pain Matters

Treating or managing pain is critical. **Unrelieved pain** can cause patients to:

a. Experience **depression**
b. Experience disruption in **activity, appetite, and sleep**
c. Feel **helpless and anxious**
d. **Give up hope**
e. **Reject treatment programs**
f. Stop participating in life to the fullest extent possible

## Three Ways to Control Pain

1. **Analgesics**
2. **Targeted medications** to control special pain problems (e.g., adjuvants)
3. **Non-medical treatments** (non-pharmacological measures)

> Treating an infection can often remove the cause of the pain so the pain medication can be stopped.

## Non-Pharmacological Measures

Some measures that help reduce pain include:

1. Massage
2. Diversionary therapy
3. Music therapy
4. Use of cold or hot applications
5. Exercise
6. Emotional support
7. Hypnosis
8. Therapeutic touch

## Four Key Principles of Pain Management with Analgesics

When using analgesics, follow **four key principles**:

1. **By the mouth** (oral route preferred)
2. **By the clock** (regular schedule, not PRN only)
3. **By the individual** (personalized dosing)
4. **By the ladder** (WHO analgesic ladder)

### 1. By the Mouth (Oral Route)

Giving analgesics by mouth is the **simplest and most reliable method** for most patients.

If the patient cannot take tablets by mouth, alternatives include:

- Subcutaneous route
- Rectal route
- Buccal route

**Intramuscular injection should be avoided.**

### 2. By the Clock

Constant pain needs regular analgesics to keep it away. Pain that is allowed to build up is more difficult to control.

- Do not wait for pain to return — give analgesics at **regular intervals** according to their duration of action.
- Painkillers should be given at a **fixed time**.
- Start at a **small dose** and adjust until the patient has no pain.
- Each dose should be given **before the effect of the previous dose wears off**.

If a patient has pain before the next scheduled dose, this is called **breakthrough pain** (see [Adjuvants & Breakthrough Pain](14-adjuvants-and-breakthrough-pain.md)).

### 3. By the Individual

Whenever possible, **patients should administer their own pain medications**. This:

- Lessens their anxiety
- Increases their sense of control

As patients become confused or too weak to self-administer, a caregiver should gradually assume control. **There is no established universal dosing for pain control** — it must be individualized.

### 4. By the Ladder (WHO Analgesic Ladder)

The **WHO analgesic ladder** gives a logical way of increasing the strength of analgesia in steps as pain increases.

#### Step 1 — Mild Pain → Non-opioid ± adjuvant

If the patient is in pain and not taking any medicine, **always start with a non-opioid**:

- Paracetamol
- Aspirin
- Ibuprofen / NSAIDs

#### Step 2 — Mild to Moderate Pain → Weak opioid + non-opioid ± adjuvant

If pain persists or increases, **add a weak opioid**:

- Codeine
- Tramadol

#### Step 3 — Moderate to Severe Pain → Strong opioid + non-opioid ± adjuvant

If pain is still not controlled or increases, **stop the codeine** and add a strong opioid:

- Oral **morphine** (added to paracetamol, aspirin, or ibuprofen)

## Common Analgesics — Dosing Reference

### Non-opioids (Step 1)

| Drug | Dose | Duration of action |
|---|---|---|
| Paracetamol | 500 mg–1 g q.d.s. | 4–6 hours |
| Aspirin | 300–600 mg q.d.s. | 4–6 hours |
| Ibuprofen | 200–400 mg t.d.s. | 6–8 hours |
| Diclofenac | 50–100 mg t.d.s. | 6–8 hours |

### Opioid analgesics

| Drug | Step | Dose | Duration of action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Codeine | Step 2 | 30–60 mg q.d.s. | 4–6 hours |
| Tramadol | Step 2 | 50–100 mg q.d.s. | 4–6 hours |
| Morphine (normal-release) | Step 3 | Starting: 2.5–5 mg q.4.h. | 4 hours |
| Morphine (sustained-release) | Step 3 | Starting: 10–20 mg q.12.h. | 12 hours |

> **Morphine has no upper dose limit** — increase dose as needed for adequate pain control.

## Key Steps in Managing Chronic Pain

1. **Select** the analgesic and the starting dose.
2. **Monitor** its effectiveness in controlling the pain.
3. **Check for side-effects** and manage them.
4. If pain is not **90% controlled after 24 hours**, **increase the dose** (if possible) or **move UP the analgesic ladder**.
5. **Full control of pain is the goal.**

## Drug Alerts ⚠️

### Morphine

Morphine may cause **respiratory depression**.

> ⚠️ If respirations are **12 or fewer breaths per minute**, **withhold medication** and contact the healthcare provider.

### NSAIDs

NSAIDs (except aspirin) have been linked to a **higher risk for cardiovascular events** such as:

- Myocardial infarction
- Stroke
- Heart failure

> ⚠️ Patients who have just had **heart surgery should not take NSAIDs**.

## Controlling Pain in the Dying Client

The **primary objective** of pain control for dying clients is to **block pain without suppressing level of consciousness or breathing**.

- Give pain medications on a **routine schedule around the clock** to avoid the cycle of intense discomfort followed by heavy sedation.
- Regular dosing sustains a **plateau of continuous pain relief** and prevents exhausting the client.
- **Reassure the client** that frequent use of narcotic analgesia will **not** cause addiction.
- The physician may prescribe **mild tranquilizers or antidepressants** to reduce fear and anxiety, since fear and anxiety can exacerbate pain.
