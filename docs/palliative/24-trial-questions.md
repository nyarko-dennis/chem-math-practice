## Assignment — Designing a Palliative Service in a District (15 marks)

> As a newly trained palliative care provider, you have been transferred to a district and you notice there was **no organised service for persons with life-threatening illness**.
>
> **Assess the situation and recommend the model of care that would be appropriate.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Conduct a situation assessment (3 marks)

Before recommending a model, gather data on:

- **Population and demography** — size, urban/rural mix, age distribution.
- **Disease burden** — prevalence of cancer, HIV/AIDS, advanced heart/lung/kidney/liver disease, advanced neurological disease, stroke. WHO data suggests >70% of advanced cancer/HIV patients have moderate-to-severe pain, so need is almost certainly significant.
- **Existing resources** — district hospital, health centres, CHPS compounds, community health workers, available essential drugs (especially **oral morphine**, paracetamol, ibuprofen, codeine), referral pathways.
- **Cultural and faith context** — local beliefs about death, dying, bereavement practices, widowhood rites.
- **Gaps** — no organized service; identify who currently dies without symptom control.

#### 2. Recommend a primary model: home-based care (3 marks)

**Home-based care** is the most appropriate primary model because:

- It is the **most common model** used in palliative care globally.
- It is **resource-light** — does not require building new inpatient facilities.
- It matches patient preference — most patients prefer to die at home, surrounded by family.
- It is culturally appropriate in many Ghanaian contexts, where family caregiving is central.
- The health worker's role is to **teach the caregiver** how to manage the patient at home (e.g., giving oral opioids on the clock and by the ladder).

#### 3. Add complementary models (2 marks)

A single model cannot cover all patients. Layer:

- **Hospital-based team** — a small palliative team based at the district hospital, going round wards to attend to terminally ill patients.
- **Outreach services** — the team travels to communities on scheduled dates for patients who cannot easily attend the hospital. Rural districts in particular benefit.
- **Outpatient care** at the hospital for patients well enough to travel.

#### 4. Team composition (2 marks)

Multidisciplinary team — note **palliative care is not nurse-only**:

- Nurses (lead implementers)
- A district medical officer
- Social worker
- Chaplain or local religious leaders (for spiritual care)
- Community health workers / volunteers
- Family caregivers

#### 5. Workforce: train existing staff (2 marks)

Specialist palliative care providers are scarce. Use the **"all healthcare providers can practice basic palliative care"** principle:

- Train existing nurses, doctors, pharmacists, community health workers in:
  - **Communication** (truth-telling, breaking bad news using BREAK NEWS)
  - **Symptom management** (especially pain — WHO analgesic ladder; nausea, dyspnea, oral care)
  - **Bereavement support**

#### 6. Scope of services to establish (3 marks)

- **Pain control** using the WHO analgesic ladder (start with paracetamol/NSAIDs → codeine → oral morphine).
- **Symptom management** beyond pain (dyspnea, nausea, anorexia, dry mouth).
- **Holistic assessment** — physical, psychological, social, cultural, spiritual (Saunders' total pain).
- **Family support** — including anticipatory grief and bereavement.
- **Culturally appropriate care** — sensitive to Ghanaian customs, widowhood rites, religious traditions.
- Set up a **simple referral pathway** to a tertiary centre for complex cases.

## Trial Question 1 — Educating a Family About Morphine (30 marks)

> Dr. Lee has prescribed **oral morphine** for a patient with severe cancer pain, but the patient's family is **hesitant due to fears of addiction**.
>
> Using the concept of **"total pain"** and the **WHO analgesic ladder**, explain how you would educate the family about pain management in palliative care.
>
> Address their concerns about addiction and discuss the importance of adequate pain control.

### Model Answer

#### 1. Acknowledge the family's concern with empathy (2 marks)

Begin by validating the family's worry. Addiction fear is **one of the most persistent misconceptions** about palliative opioid use, shared by patients and families worldwide. Sit at their level, use plain language, avoid medical jargon. Reassure them that their concern shows their love for the patient.

#### 2. Introduce the concept of Total Pain (Saunders) (5 marks)

Explain that pain in advanced cancer is **never just physical**. Dame Cicely Saunders described pain as five overlapping dimensions:

- **Physical** — tissue damage from the tumour (e.g., bone metastases, organ invasion).
- **Psychological** — anxiety, depression, anger, fear of death.
- **Social** — isolation, financial worries, being a burden on family.
- **Cultural** — language, beliefs, lifestyle disruption.
- **Spiritual** — questions of meaning, guilt, "What is there to live for?", "Will God forgive me?"

Each dimension worsens the others — pain feels worse when patients are anxious or alone. Effective pain relief therefore **needs morphine for the physical component AND attention to the other four** through counselling, family support, social work, and chaplaincy.

#### 3. Explain the WHO Analgesic Ladder (5 marks)

- **Step 1** — non-opioids (paracetamol, aspirin, ibuprofen) for mild pain.
- **Step 2** — weak opioid (codeine or tramadol) added when pain persists.
- **Step 3** — strong opioid (morphine), replacing the weak opioid but still alongside the non-opioid, for moderate-to-severe pain.

Severe cancer pain requires Step 3. **Morphine is the gold standard** strong opioid and is recommended by WHO.

#### 4. Apply the four key principles (3 marks)

- **By the mouth** — oral morphine is preferred; effective and simple.
- **By the clock** — given at fixed intervals so pain never builds up. Each dose is given before the previous wears off.
- **By the individual** — dose is titrated to the patient's pain. There is **no universal dose**.
- **By the ladder** — increase systematically as pain demands.

#### 5. Address the addiction fear directly (5 marks)

- When prescribed appropriately and monitored by trained professionals, **addiction is extremely rare** in patients receiving opioids for cancer pain.
- The **goal is to relieve suffering**, not to induce dependency.
- Distinguish **physical dependence and tolerance** (normal physiological adaptations — not the same as addiction) from **addiction** (compulsive use despite harm — almost unheard of in supervised palliative use).
- Morphine has **no upper dose limit** — the dose is increased as needed for pain control.
- The patient will not "become a different person" — pain relief usually allows them to be **more themselves**, not less.

#### 6. Explain the consequences of unrelieved pain (4 marks)

Unrelieved pain causes patients to:

- Experience depression and hopelessness
- Lose appetite and sleep
- Feel helpless and anxious
- **Give up hope**
- Reject further treatment
- Withdraw from family and life

In other words, withholding morphine to "protect" the patient actually **harms** them and shortens meaningful time with the family.

#### 7. Reassure on safety and breakthrough pain (3 marks)

- The nurse and doctor will monitor for side effects — constipation is common but treatable (laxatives); nausea usually settles in days.
- **Respiratory depression** is rare at therapeutic doses; the team will monitor respiratory rate (withhold if ≤12 breaths/min).
- For **breakthrough pain** (sudden flare before the next dose), an extra rescue dose — usually **half the regular dose** — will be given without disrupting the schedule.

#### 8. Invite family partnership (3 marks)

- Encourage the family to support the schedule (the patient takes morphine even when not in pain, to prevent pain from returning).
- Empower them to **report concerns** — new pains, side effects, emotional changes — so the team can adjust.
- Reassure that the team will be present throughout: *"We are always here to help if new problems arise."*

## Trial Question 2 — Inadequate Pain Control at WHO Step 3 (20 marks)

> Sarah, a palliative care nurse, is caring for a patient whose pain is **not well-controlled despite being on Step 3** of the WHO analgesic ladder.
>
> Analyze potential reasons for inadequate pain control in this scenario.
>
> What **adjuvant medications or non-pharmacological interventions** might Sarah consider to improve pain management?

### Model Answer

#### 1. Reassess the pain comprehensively (3 marks)

Sarah's first action is **not** to immediately add drugs, but to re-assess:

- Use **PQRST** or **OLDCART** to characterize the pain afresh — onset, location, duration, character, intensity (0–10), relief measures.
- **Patient self-report** remains the most reliable indicator.
- Look for a **change in mechanism** — has the pain become neuropathic? Is there a new lesion (e.g., bone metastasis, nerve compression)?
- Assess all five dimensions of **total pain** — is psychological, social, or spiritual distress amplifying the physical pain?

#### 2. Possible reasons for inadequate control (5 marks)

- **Dose too low** — morphine has no upper dose limit; the patient may simply need a higher dose. If pain is not 90% controlled after 24 hours, increase the dose.
- **Wrong analgesic for the mechanism** — opioids alone are often insufficient for **neuropathic pain** (burning, shooting, electric-shock-like). An adjuvant is needed.
- **Missing breakthrough doses** — if the patient experiences regular flare-ups, the baseline dose is too low and/or rescue doses are not being given.
- **Tolerance** — dose may need titration upward.
- **Non-physical dimensions untreated** — anxiety, depression, spiritual distress, family conflict are amplifying perceived pain.
- **Compliance issues** — patient skipping doses due to side effects or addiction fears.
- **New pathology** — disease progression with new pain source.

#### 3. Adjuvant medications to consider (5 marks)

Adjuvants are drugs not originally designed as analgesics but useful for specific pain types. They can be added **at any step** of the ladder.

| Pain type | Adjuvant | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Neuropathic** (burning, shooting) | Tricyclic antidepressants | Amitriptyline, imipramine |
| **Neuropathic** | Anticonvulsants | Gabapentin, valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin |
| **Inflammatory / swelling** (e.g., raised intracranial pressure, nerve compression) | Corticosteroids | Dexamethasone, prednisolone |
| **Skeletal muscle spasm** | Benzodiazepines | Diazepam, lorazepam |
| **Smooth muscle spasm** (e.g., abdominal colic) | Anticholinergics | Hyoscine butylbromide |

#### 4. Non-pharmacological interventions (4 marks)

These can be used alongside drugs at any stage:

- **Massage**
- **Heat or cold applications**
- **Music therapy** and **diversionary therapy** (distraction)
- **Therapeutic touch**
- **Hypnosis**
- **Gentle exercise** within tolerance
- **Emotional support and presence**
- **Positioning** — Fowler's position, repositioning to relieve pressure

These work in part by modulating pain at the brain level — distraction directs attention away from the sensory and affective components of pain.

#### 5. Address Total Pain holistically (3 marks)

- **Psychological** — counselling for anxiety/depression; consider TCAs (dual benefit — neuropathic adjuvant and antidepressant) or SNRIs.
- **Social** — involve social worker to address family conflict, finances, isolation.
- **Spiritual** — chaplaincy referral if patient or family is open to it.
- **Cultural** — ensure care respects the patient's beliefs and rituals.

Without addressing these, even maximum opioid doses may not relieve perceived pain.

## Trial Question 3 — Supporting a Family Through Worden's Tasks (20 marks)

> John, a hospice nurse, is caring for a patient in the final stages of life. The patient's family is struggling to cope with the impending loss.
>
> Using **Worden's "tasks of mourning" framework**, describe how John can support the family through each task, **both before and after the patient's death**.
>
> Provide specific examples of **interventions or conversations** John might have with the family.

### Model Answer

#### Introduction: Worden's framework (2 marks)

Worden identifies **four tasks of mourning**. They are a fluctuating, non-linear process — not a strict sequence. Effective bereavement support **begins before death** (anticipatory grief) and continues afterwards.

#### Task 1 — Accept the reality of the loss (4 marks)

**Before death:**

- Have **honest, gentle conversations** about the prognosis. Use the **BREAK NEWS** framework. Example: *"We need to sit down and talk — things are quite serious. The cancer has spread, and the treatments we have are no longer working."*
- Help the family confront the **finality of the situation** without removing all hope: *"We cannot cure this, but we can help her be comfortable."*
- Allow time for the news to settle. Avoid false reassurance.

**After death:**

- Encourage the family to **see and spend time with the body** if culturally appropriate.
- Support participation in **funeral and memorial rituals**, which help confront finality.
- Use simple, clear language ("died," not "passed away" if it adds ambiguity).

#### Task 2 — Experience (acknowledge) the pain of grief (5 marks)

**Before death (anticipatory grief):**

- Create a **safe space** for family members to express feelings. Sit, lean forward, make eye contact, allow silence.
- **Normalize** their emotions: *"It is normal to feel sad, angry, or scared right now."*
- Use empathy: *"This must be very difficult for you to watch."*
- Do not try to stop their tears — crying is a normal, helpful reaction.

**After death:**

- Validate the full range of grief manifestations — **affective** (sadness, anger, guilt, anxiety), **physical** (chest tightness, breathlessness, dry mouth, fatigue), **cognitive** (disbelief, preoccupation, even hallucinations of the deceased), and **behavioural** (sleep/appetite disturbance, social withdrawal).
- Reassure them that **upsurges of grief** on birthdays, anniversaries, and significant days are normal.
- Watch for **dysfunctional grief** — denial >6 months, severe physical symptoms, suicidal ideation — and refer appropriately.

#### Task 3 — Adjust to a new environment without the deceased (4 marks)

**Before death:**

- Help the family **anticipate practical changes** — who will manage finances, household tasks, childcare, decisions previously made by the patient?
- Discuss role changes openly. Example: *"Have you thought about how you will manage the children's school fees once your husband is no longer here?"*
- Identify support networks early.

**After death:**

- This task often begins **several months after** the death.
- Support the survivor in developing **new skills** — managing the home alone, taking a job, parenting solo.
- Connect them with **community resources** — support groups, financial counselling, practical help.
- Provide **respite for primary caregivers** who may have lost their day-to-day role.

#### Task 4 — Reinvest energy from the deceased into new life (3 marks)

**Before death:**

- Less directly applicable, but the foundation is laid by helping the family **say goodbye well** — completing unfinished business, expressing love, granting "permission" to die ("waiting for permission" phenomenon).

**After death:**

- Encourage gradual re-engagement with life. Reassure that **forming new relationships or interests does not betray the deceased**.
- Support activities that **honour the deceased's memory** — e.g., advocacy work for the disease the patient died of, charitable giving, memorial events.
- Recognize that grief does not have a fixed end — the pain becomes "less intense and more manageable," but **never fully disappears**.

#### General support principles John should follow (2 marks)

- **Listen non-judgmentally.**
- **Reach out** to the bereaved by visiting and expressing sympathy.
- **Reassure** them they did everything possible — they did not cause the death.
- **Educate** about normal grief responses.
- **Respect cultural and religious rituals** — encourage the family to use the rituals that symbolize transition, continuity, and healing for them.

## Trial Question 4 — Mrs. Mariama Abu Case Study (30 marks)

> Mrs. Mariama Abu is a 35-year-old trader admitted to the Medical-Surgical Emergency with severe pain, swelling of the right breast, and difficulty sleeping.
>
> On examination, the **left breast had been removed**. She also complains of **difficulty breathing** and has **lost appetite**.
>
> Further assessment reveals **terminal cancer with bone metastasis**.
>
> While interacting with **Baba Abu (husband)**, you realize he is **aware of Mrs. Abu's prognosis**, but Mrs. Abu **seems unaware of her condition**. Baba Abu believes that **it is better if she is not informed**.
>
> **How will you care for Mrs. Abu at the end of life?**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Comprehensive holistic assessment (3 marks)

Approach Mrs. Abu with a **holistic, total-pain framework**:

- **Physical** — severe pain (bone metastases — typically deep, aching, may have neuropathic component if nerve compression), dyspnea, anorexia, insomnia, possible disfigurement from mastectomy.
- **Psychological** — fear, anxiety, body image distress from breast loss.
- **Social** — she is 35, likely with young dependents; financial concerns as a trader unable to work; relationship with husband; community role.
- **Cultural** — Ghanaian context; widowhood and family rituals; gender dynamics in disclosure.
- **Spiritual** — meaning-making, questions about why she is suffering, faith.

Begin **frequent but unintrusive observations** so she feels secure and not abandoned.

#### 2. Pain management — bone metastasis pain (4 marks)

Bone metastases produce severe deep somatic pain, often poorly controlled by opioids alone.

- **WHO Step 3** strong opioid — **oral morphine**, starting 2.5–5 mg every 4 hours and titrate upward (no dose limit).
- **By the clock** dosing; rescue dose (~½ the regular dose) for breakthrough pain.
- Combine with **non-opioid** (paracetamol or NSAID — but caution with NSAIDs given cardiovascular risk; avoid post-heart-surgery).
- Add an **adjuvant**:
  - **Corticosteroids** (dexamethasone) — reduce peri-tumour inflammation contributing to bone pain.
  - **Bisphosphonates / referral to oncology for palliative radiotherapy** if available.
  - **TCAs or anticonvulsants** if neuropathic component is identified.
- **Non-pharmacological** measures — gentle massage, heat/cold, music therapy, positioning, therapeutic touch.
- Monitor respiratory rate (withhold morphine if ≤12 breaths/min).

#### 3. Manage dyspnea and other physical symptoms (3 marks)

- **Difficulty breathing:** place in **Fowler's position**; gentle suctioning if she cannot clear secretions; oxygen if needed. A sedative may be prescribed if anxiety from breathlessness is severe.
- **Anorexia:** offer **small, frequent meals** of foods she likes or family traditional foods; administer antiemetics about **1 hour before meals** if nausea is present; do not insist on eating if symptoms cannot be controlled.
- **Insomnia:** cluster nursing care to avoid awakening her; dim lights at night; play her favourite music; control pain (a major cause of sleeplessness).
- **Oral care:** ice chips and **petroleum jelly** for dry lips (avoid glycerin — it worsens drying).
- **Skin integrity:** reposition at least every 2 hours.

#### 4. The ethical conflict — disclosure (5 marks)

This is the central dilemma. The relevant principles:

- **Autonomy** — Mrs. Abu has the right to know her diagnosis to make decisions about her remaining time.
- **Veracity (truth-telling)** — core ethical principle; lying destroys trust.
- **Beneficence** — what is truly in her best interest?
- **Cultural sensitivity** — in many cultures, families wish to "protect" the patient from bad news; this must be acknowledged respectfully.

The literature is clear: studies show **patients usually want to know the truth and cope better when they do**. Many patients suspect their condition anyway — Mrs. Abu may already sense something is seriously wrong.

#### 5. Counsel Baba Abu (3 marks)

- **Acknowledge his concern** as an expression of love and protection.
- Counsel him gently on the benefits of truth-telling:
  - Maintains trust between him and his wife
  - Reduces her isolation and uncertainty
  - Allows her to make decisions about her children's future, her business, and any spiritual matters
  - Allows them to share remaining time meaningfully and say goodbye
- Reassure him that **she will not be told without him present**, and that the team will deliver the news gently and at her pace.
- Use the **BREAK NEWS** framework when the conversation happens.

#### 6. Apply BREAK NEWS when disclosing (3 marks)

- **B**e prepared — quiet space, no interruptions.
- **R**elatives — Baba Abu present.
- **E**xpectations — *"Have you been wondering what is happening to you? What have the doctors told you so far?"*
- **A**ssess — how much does she want to know today?
- **K**nowledge sharing — slowly, gently, plain language. Warning shot: *"Things are quite serious."*
- **N**ever say "there is nothing we can do" — emphasize: *"We have medicine that will control your pain and help your breathing."*
- **E**mpathize — *"This must be very difficult to hear."* Allow tears; do not rush.
- **W**ay forward — outline the care plan; fix a follow-up time.
- **S**top and reflect afterwards.

#### 7. Psychosocial and spiritual care (3 marks)

- Address her anxieties about her children's future — involve social worker; explore family or community arrangements; help her articulate her wishes.
- Address financial worries — her husband and possibly extended family will need support.
- Counsel her on fears of pain, loneliness, and dying.
- Offer **spiritual care** — explore her faith; involve clergy or a religious figure if she wishes.
- Allow her to engage in **life review** — talking about her life, accomplishments, hopes.

#### 8. Cultural sensitivity (2 marks)

- Respect Ghanaian customs around dying and bereavement (e.g., presence of extended family, traditional rituals).
- Use **culturally appropriate language**.
- Support the family to perform and complete cultural rituals.
- Be aware of widowhood rites that may follow her death and prepare to support Baba Abu.
- Engage local clergy and community elders if helpful.

#### 9. Family support and preparation (2 marks)

- Provide a private space for family conversation.
- Prepare Baba Abu and the family for the **physical changes** of dying (mottling, Cheyne-Stokes breathing, "death rattle," detachment).
- Recognize the **"waiting for permission" phenomenon** — Mrs. Abu may linger until the family signals readiness to let her go. Reassure them this is not abandonment.

#### 10. Continuity and bereavement (2 marks)

- Coordinate with the interdisciplinary team (doctor, social worker, chaplain, pharmacist).
- Document a clear care plan and pass on at handover.
- Plan **bereavement follow-up** for the family — palliative care does not end at death.

## Case 5 — Counselling a Family on Palliative Care Misconceptions (15 marks)

> Mr. Owusu's 67-year-old mother has advanced lung cancer. The doctor has recommended palliative care. Mr. Owusu refuses, saying *"Palliative care means giving up — it means you have stopped fighting for her, and the morphine will make her an addict."*
>
> **Counsel Mr. Owusu on the meaning, scope, and benefits of palliative care.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Acknowledge the concern empathically (2 marks)

Begin by validating his love for his mother. His resistance is rooted in care, not ignorance. Sit at his level, use plain language, and reassure him that his concerns will be addressed.

#### 2. Define palliative care correctly (3 marks)

Per **WHO (2005)**, palliative care is the **active holistic care** of patients whose disease is not responsive to curative treatment. Emphasise:

- It is **not abandonment** — it is intensive, proactive care.
- It begins **at diagnosis**, not at the moment of death.
- It can **coexist with curative treatment** (e.g., chemotherapy, radiotherapy).
- It addresses **physical, psychological, social, cultural, and spiritual** needs of patient AND family.

#### 3. Correct each misconception specifically (4 marks)

- **"Giving up hope"** — Palliative care does not abandon hope. It focuses on **comfort, dignity, and quality of life**, and can run alongside curative therapy. Many patients live longer and better with good symptom control.
- **"Addiction to morphine"** — When prescribed appropriately and monitored, **addiction is extremely rare**. The goal is to relieve suffering, not induce dependency. Morphine is the **WHO-recommended** strong opioid for severe cancer pain.
- **"Only for the dying"** — Palliative care is for **anyone with a life-limiting illness** — cancer, heart failure, COPD, kidney/liver failure, HIV, neurological disease.

#### 4. Describe what palliative care will actually do for his mother (4 marks)

- **Pain and symptom control** — pain, breathlessness, nausea, sleep disturbance, anorexia.
- **Emotional support** — counselling for fear, anxiety, depression.
- **Practical help** — guidance on finances, transport, forms.
- **Spiritual care** — chaplaincy if she wishes.
- **Family support** — including bereavement follow-up after death.

#### 5. Reassure on safety and team involvement (2 marks)

The team — nurses, doctor, social worker, chaplain, pharmacist — will:

- Monitor for side effects (respiratory rate, constipation).
- Adjust the regimen as needed.
- Involve Mr. Owusu and the family in every decision.

## Case 6 — Choosing a Model of Care for a Patient Declining Further Treatment (15 marks)

> Mrs. Asantewaa, 58, has end-stage ovarian cancer. After two lines of chemotherapy, she tells her oncologist she no longer wants curative treatment. Her prognosis is approximately 4 months. She lives in a small town 2 hours from the regional hospital and wants to remain with her family.
>
> **Recommend an appropriate model of care, justifying your choice. Explain the distinction between hospice care and palliative care in her context.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Distinguish hospice from palliative care (3 marks)

- **Palliative care** can be received **alongside** curative treatments.
- **Hospice care** is provided **once the patient forgoes curative treatment** and has a prognosis typically **≤6 months**.
- Mrs. Asantewaa qualifies for **hospice-level care** — she has declined further curative therapy and has a 4-month prognosis.
- Hospice is **not a place but a concept of care** — it can be delivered at home, in inpatient settings, or in long-term care.

#### 2. Recommend primary model: home-based hospice care (4 marks)

This is the most appropriate primary model because:

- It is the **most common model** of palliative/hospice delivery.
- The patient explicitly wishes to stay with her family.
- It is consistent with her cultural preference and prognosis.
- Home is 2 hours from the hospital — daily visits there are not realistic.
- The team will **teach the family caregivers** to administer oral morphine on the clock and by the ladder.

#### 3. Add complementary models (3 marks)

- **Outreach services** — palliative team visits the community on scheduled dates for review and resupply.
- **Hospital-based** support — for inpatient stays if symptoms become uncontrollable at home, or for procedures (e.g., paracentesis).
- **Day care** at the regional hospital may be useful occasionally for respite or social contact.

#### 4. Team and scope (3 marks)

- **Team** — nurses (lead), district medical officer, social worker, chaplain/local religious figure, family caregivers, community volunteers.
- **Scope** — pain control via WHO ladder; symptom management (dyspnea, nausea, anorexia, oral care); psychosocial and spiritual care; bereavement follow-up.

#### 5. Emphasise quality over quantity (2 marks)

Reassure Mrs. Asantewaa and the family that hospice care emphasises **quality of life rather than quantity** — helping her live as fully and comfortably as possible until death.

## Case 7 — Autonomy vs. Family Wishes (20 marks)

> Mr. Tetteh, 72, is a competent patient with end-stage heart failure. He has clearly stated to the team that he **does not want a feeding tube** if he can no longer swallow. His adult children are insisting that the team insert one because *"we cannot watch him starve."*
>
> **How do you resolve this ethical dilemma? Apply the principles of palliative care ethics.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Identify the conflict (2 marks)

The conflict is between the patient's expressed wishes (Mr. Tetteh refuses a feeding tube) and family wishes (children demand intervention). The clinician must navigate this while honouring core ethical principles.

#### 2. Apply Autonomy (4 marks)

- **Autonomy** is the patient's right to make decisions about their own care, including refusing treatment.
- Mr. Tetteh is **competent** — his decision must be respected.
- Even patients near death **retain the right to consent or refuse** specific interventions.
- The family does not override the competent patient's autonomy.

#### 3. Apply Beneficence and Nonmaleficence (4 marks)

- **Beneficence** — act in the patient's best interest. For a dying patient, this may mean **comfort over prolongation**.
- **Nonmaleficence** — do no harm. Forcing a feeding tube against the patient's wishes is **harmful** because:
  - It violates his bodily autonomy.
  - It can cause aspiration pneumonia, agitation, infection.
  - It does not reverse the underlying disease.
- A feeding tube does not "stop starvation" — anorexia at end of life is a **physiological process**, not hunger.

#### 4. Apply Veracity (Truth-telling) (2 marks)

- Be honest with the family about what a feeding tube will and will not do.
- Explain that GI function slows naturally at end of life; forcing feeding can cause distention, nausea, and aspiration.

#### 5. Counsel the family compassionately (4 marks)

- Acknowledge their fear — watching a parent stop eating feels like causing their death.
- Use the **BREAK NEWS** principles for difficult conversations.
- Frame the patient's wishes as an act of dignity, not abandonment.
- Reassure them that the team will keep him comfortable — mouth care with ice chips and petroleum jelly, gentle hygiene, presence.
- Offer **family meetings** to allow them to express their feelings.
- Engage **chaplaincy** if appropriate.

#### 6. Document and team approach (2 marks)

- Document Mr. Tetteh's wishes clearly in the record.
- Communicate the decision across the multidisciplinary team.
- Provide bereavement follow-up — the family will need support after his death.

#### 7. Respect the principle: presence is everything (2 marks)

> *"Presence is everything when end of life is approaching. Watch with your patient."*

The intervention the family really wants is not the tube — it is **not to feel helpless**. Help them sit with him, hold his hand, play music, and say what they need to say.

## Case 8 — Truth-Telling with a Child (15 marks)

> Kwame, 10, has end-stage leukaemia. During a quiet moment he asks you: *"Nurse, am I going to die?"* His parents have told the team they do not want him to be told the truth — *"He is too young."*
>
> **How do you respond? Apply ethical and communication principles.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Recognise that children know more than adults assume (3 marks)

- Children often **understand far more about their situation than we realise** — they sense anxiety, overhear conversations, see disease in other children on the ward.
- **Hearing the truth can be a relief** — it may not be as bad as their secret fears.
- The principle "we will not give information to the child without parent permission, **but we will not lie**" is the right starting position.

#### 2. Reflect the question back (3 marks)

Do not answer immediately. Use reflective questions to find out what he knows and is most worried about:

- *"I wonder what made you ask that question?"*
- *"Have you talked to Mummy or Daddy about it? What did they say?"*
- *"Tell me what you think first, then I will tell you what I think."*

This honours both his parents' position **and** his right not to be deceived.

#### 3. Negotiate with the parents (3 marks)

- Counsel them gently: **studies show families cope better with openness than secrecy**.
- Explain that children sense the truth — withholding it leads to isolation and silent fear.
- Reassure them that any disclosure will be **age-appropriate, gradual, and led by them** if they wish.
- Ultimately, decisions about disclosure rest with parents — but you may say: *"I will not lie to him. If he asks me directly, I will redirect to you."*

#### 4. Apply ethical principles (3 marks)

- **Veracity** — never lie; lying destroys trust.
- **Autonomy** — children are people with opinions to be heard; they can make choices when supported.
- **Beneficence** — telling the truth often relieves the child's fears and allows preparation.
- **Nonmaleficence** — forcing too much information at once can harm; reflection prevents this.

#### 5. Use child-appropriate communication (3 marks)

- **Get down to his level** — sit beside the bed.
- **Direct eye contact may feel threatening** — talk while playing or drawing.
- **Be respectful and polite** — address him directly, not just his parents.
- **Avoid medical jargon**.
- **Allow questions** and the freedom to say *"I don't want to talk about it right now."*

## Case 9 — Applying Total Pain to an HIV-Positive Widow (20 marks)

> Akosua, 32, is a widow with three young children. Her husband died of AIDS-related illness six months ago. She has just been diagnosed with advanced cervical cancer. She has severe pelvic pain, a foul-smelling vaginal discharge, and difficulty sleeping. She has no income and her neighbours have begun avoiding her, saying *"the family is cursed."* She tells you, *"God has abandoned me. What is there left to live for?"*
>
> **Apply Saunders' concept of "Total Pain" to assess and plan her care.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Introduce the concept of total pain (2 marks)

Dame Cicely Saunders described pain as five overlapping dimensions: **physical, psychological, social, cultural, and spiritual**. Each amplifies the others. Treating only the physical pain will leave Akosua suffering.

#### 2. Physical pain (4 marks)

- **Source** — pelvic pain from tumour invasion; possible nerve involvement.
- **Symptoms** — severe pain, sleep loss, malodorous discharge, possible fatigue.
- **Plan:**
  - **WHO Step 3** opioid — oral morphine, titrated.
  - **Adjuvants** — anticonvulsant or TCA if neuropathic component (nerve compression).
  - **Wound care** — gentle hygiene, deodorising dressings.
  - **Treat any infection** — may reduce pain at its source.
  - **Sleep promotion** — control pain first; cluster care; quiet environment.

#### 3. Psychological pain (4 marks)

- Akosua likely feels **fear, depression, hopelessness**, possibly anger.
- **Plan:**
  - **Counselling** — let her express feelings without judgement; use active listening.
  - **Antidepressant** if depression is significant — TCAs serve dual purpose (mood + neuropathic pain).
  - **Support groups** if available — meeting others who have shared the experience can reduce isolation.

#### 4. Social pain (4 marks)

- **Stigma and discrimination** — neighbours avoid her; cultural blame ("cursed").
- **Practical deprivation** — no income; three dependents.
- **Plan:**
  - **Social worker referral** — to access any available financial assistance, food support, school fees for children.
  - **Connect with community-based HIV/cancer support groups** — peer support reduces isolation.
  - Help her **plan for her children's future** — who will care for them; involve extended family or community where possible.

#### 5. Cultural pain (3 marks)

- The community's belief that she is "cursed" reflects cultural stigma.
- **Plan:**
  - Approach with **cultural humility** — do not dismiss community beliefs.
  - **Engage local respected figures** (elders, chaplains) if helpful to reduce stigma.
  - Use **appropriate language and terminology**.
  - Support her in maintaining cultural practices that bring meaning (e.g., dress, food, rituals).

#### 6. Spiritual pain (3 marks)

- "God has abandoned me. What is there left to live for?" — classic spiritual distress.
- **Plan:**
  - **Chaplaincy or spiritual care referral** — if she wishes.
  - **Listen** to her spiritual pain without imposing your beliefs.
  - Help her **explore meaning** — her children, the love she gave her husband, the work she has done.
  - Pray with her **if she requests it** — never impose.

## Case 10 — Pain Assessment in a Non-Verbal Patient (15 marks)

> Mr. Mensah, 78, is in his final days. He has advanced dementia and cannot self-report. His daughter believes he is in pain — he grimaces and pulls his legs up when repositioned. The ward team says *"his vital signs are normal, he is fine."*
>
> **Describe how you would assess his pain and respond to the team's dismissal.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Reject the false reassurance (3 marks)

- **Vital signs (HR, BP) are not reliable indicators of pain** — this is a core principle of pain assessment.
- Absence of tachycardia does not mean absence of pain.
- **Pain can exist even when no physical sign can be found.**
- Dismissing the daughter's observation is poor practice — **include family in assessment** when self-report is unavailable.

#### 2. Use proxy / behavioural assessment (4 marks)

When the patient cannot self-report, use **proxy measurement** based on:

- **Facial expression** — grimacing, frowning, clenched jaw.
- **Vocalizations** — moaning, groaning, crying out.
- **Body movements** — guarding, pulling legs up, restlessness, rigidity.
- **Changes in interpersonal interactions** — withdrawal, aggression.
- **Changes in activity pattern** — sleep disturbance, refusing care.
- **Mental status changes** — increased confusion, delirium.

Mr. Mensah's **grimacing and leg-pulling on repositioning are strong behavioural signs of pain**.

#### 3. Use a validated proxy tool (2 marks)

Use a behavioural pain scale appropriate to the population (e.g., a face-based or behavioural observation scale). Document findings consistently across shifts so changes can be tracked.

#### 4. Trial of analgesia (3 marks)

If pain is suspected and assessment supports it, a **trial of analgesia** is an appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic step:

- Start with paracetamol regularly (Step 1).
- If signs persist, escalate per WHO ladder.
- Reassess after each dose for behavioural change.

#### 5. Family and team education (3 marks)

- Acknowledge the daughter's observation as **valuable clinical information**.
- Educate the team: physiological signs are not reliable; self-report is the gold standard; in its absence, behaviour is.
- Document the assessment, plan, and response so the team approach is consistent.

## Case 11 — Frequent Breakthrough Pain on Stable Morphine (20 marks)

> Mr. Boateng has bone metastases from prostate cancer. He is on **oral morphine 20 mg every 4 hours** with paracetamol. He is having **3 to 4 episodes of severe breakthrough pain per day**, particularly when he moves. His rescue dose is currently 5 mg.
>
> **Analyse his pain regimen and propose adjustments.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Reassess the pain (3 marks)

- Use **PQRST or OLDCART** to characterize the breakthrough episodes.
- Bone metastases produce **deep, aching pain**, often **incident pain** triggered by movement.
- Look for a **neuropathic component** (burning, shooting pain) — suggests nerve compression.
- Confirm there is no new pathology (fracture, new lesion).

#### 2. Identify the inadequate rescue dose (3 marks)

- Standard rescue dose is **half of the regular scheduled dose**.
- Mr. Boateng's regular dose is 20 mg → **rescue should be ~10 mg**, not 5 mg.
- **Increase rescue dose to 10 mg.**

#### 3. Increase the baseline dose (3 marks)

- Frequent breakthrough pain (3–4 episodes/day) is a clear sign that **baseline analgesia is inadequate**.
- Morphine has **no upper dose limit** — titrate up.
- A reasonable step is to **increase scheduled dose to 30 mg every 4 hours** and reassess in 24 hours.
- Move up the ladder if pain is not 90% controlled after 24 hours of titration.

#### 4. Add a bone-pain adjuvant (4 marks)

Bone-mets pain has an inflammatory component that opioids alone do not fully address:

- **Corticosteroid** — dexamethasone — reduces peri-tumour inflammation and bone pain.
- **NSAID** — paracetamol alone is weak for bone pain; ibuprofen or diclofenac is more effective (caution: cardiovascular risk; avoid if recent heart surgery).
- **Refer to oncology** for palliative **radiotherapy** if available — single-fraction radiotherapy is highly effective for localized bone metastases.
- **Bisphosphonates** may be considered.

#### 5. Address incident pain specifically (3 marks)

Incident pain (movement-triggered):

- Give a **pre-emptive rescue dose 30 minutes before** activities known to provoke pain (e.g., morning bath, physiotherapy).
- Use **gentle handling techniques** and assistive devices.
- **Position changes every 2 hours** with adequate analgesia.

#### 6. Non-pharmacological measures (2 marks)

- **Massage** of unaffected areas, **heat or cold** as tolerated.
- **Music therapy, distraction, therapeutic touch** to modulate perception.
- **Emotional support** — fear of pain on movement itself causes anxiety, amplifying pain.

#### 7. Monitor for opioid side effects (2 marks)

- **Respiratory rate** — withhold morphine if ≤12 breaths/min.
- **Constipation** — prophylactic laxatives (almost universal need).
- **Nausea** — antiemetics, usually self-limits in days.
- **Sedation** — monitor; usually wanes after a few days.

## Case 12 — Preparing a Family for the Physical Process of Dying (20 marks)

> Mrs. Adjei's adult children have gathered at her bedside in her final hours. They ask: *"What will happen now? How will we know? What should we do?"*
>
> **Describe how you would prepare and support them through the dying process.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Set the frame: dying is a process, not a moment (2 marks)

Reassure the family that **dying is a natural process** — the body shuts down in stages. Most of what they will see is **expected and not painful for the patient**. Their presence matters more than any intervention.

#### 2. Sensory changes (2 marks)

- **Hearing is usually the last sense to disappear.** Encourage them to **keep speaking gently** to her — tell her they love her, say goodbye, even when she seems unresponsive.
- Sight blurs; eyelids may remain half-open; blink reflex disappears.

#### 3. Breathing changes (3 marks)

- **Cheyne–Stokes respiration** — alternating periods of apnea and deep, rapid breathing. Looks dramatic but is normal.
- **"Death rattle"** — noisy, gurgling breathing from secretions she cannot clear. It is **distressing to hear but not painful for her**.
- **Terminal gasping** — irregular slowing breaths near the end.
- Reassurance: she is not "drowning" or "choking" — these are reflex changes.

#### 4. Cardiovascular and skin changes (2 marks)

- **Mottling** on hands, feet, knees; cold, clammy skin; cyanosis of nose and nail beds.
- Pulse weakens and becomes irregular.
- Skin may appear "waxlike" very near death.

#### 5. Musculoskeletal and GI changes (2 marks)

- **Jaw sags**, swallowing becomes difficult, gag reflex is lost.
- **Incontinence** of bowel and bladder may occur — provide pads, dignity, hygiene.
- A bowel movement may occur at or near the time of death.

#### 6. Psychological and behavioural changes (3 marks)

- **Withdrawal** and decreased socialization — not rejection.
- **Restlessness** or **peacefulness**.
- **Unusual communication** — she may reach out, point, or call names of deceased loved ones (**nearing-death awareness**). Treat this as meaningful, not confused.
- She may **"wait for permission"** — death often comes shortly after the family signals readiness to let her go. Reassure them this is not abandonment.

#### 7. What the family can DO (3 marks)

- **Stay present.** Sit close, hold her hand, speak softly.
- **Play her favourite music** if she enjoyed it.
- **Tell her what she has meant to them** — these conversations are precious.
- **Take turns to rest** — vigil can be long; you need to look after yourselves.
- **Allow tears** — they are normal and helpful.
- If they wish, **involve clergy** for prayer or rituals.

#### 8. What the nurse will do (2 marks)

- Frequent unobtrusive checks so she is comfortable but not over-stimulated.
- **Reposition every 2 hours**, oral care with petroleum jelly and ice chips.
- **Pain medication around the clock**, monitoring respiratory rate.
- **Cluster care** to promote sleep; dim lights at night.

#### 9. Bereavement preparation (1 mark)

Remind them that the team will be there **after** her death as well — bereavement support and any practical assistance.

## Case 13 — Sudden Terminal Restlessness (20 marks)

> Mr. Quaye has been peaceful on the palliative pathway for two days. Suddenly he becomes **restless and agitated**, pulling at his sheets and trying to climb out of bed. His respirations are noisy and shallow. The family are distressed.
>
> **How do you assess and manage this situation?**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Recognise terminal restlessness (3 marks)

- Restlessness, agitation, and pulling at sheets are **specific psychosocial manifestations of approaching death**.
- It can also be caused by **reversible factors** that should be screened for, not assumed.

#### 2. Rapid systematic assessment for reversible causes (5 marks)

Quickly screen for treatable causes before assuming it is terminal:

- **Pain** — undertreated pain commonly presents as agitation in patients who cannot articulate it. Reassess using behavioural signs.
- **Urinary retention** — full bladder. Palpate and catheterise if needed.
- **Faecal impaction** — examine and treat with gentle enema if appropriate.
- **Dyspnea** — air hunger from poor ventilation.
- **Hypoxia** — consider oxygen if available.
- **Opioid side effects** — myoclonus and confusion in patients on **large doses of opioids**.
- **Medication review** — recently added drugs that may cause delirium.
- **Full bladder, full bowel, unmet need for repositioning** — quick checks.

#### 3. Address reversible causes first (3 marks)

- **Pain** — give breakthrough dose; titrate up if needed.
- **Urinary retention** — catheterise.
- **Bowel impaction** — manage.
- **Position** — Fowler's position for breathing comfort.
- **Reposition** to relieve pressure if not done recently.

#### 4. Manage the noisy "death rattle" (2 marks)

- **Reposition** — turn to the side to allow secretions to drain.
- **Gentle suction** if accumulated secretions are at the back of the throat (do not deep-suction — increases distress).
- An **anticholinergic** (e.g., hyoscine butylbromide) may be prescribed to reduce secretions.
- Reassure family the sound is **not painful for the patient**.

#### 5. Pharmacological management of irreversible agitation (3 marks)

If reversible causes are addressed and restlessness persists, this is **terminal agitation**:

- Per the source: physician may prescribe **mild tranquilizers** to reduce fear and anxiety, which can themselves exacerbate distress.
- A sedative may also relieve the **anxiety created by the feeling of suffocation** in patients with pulmonary edema.
- Goal: **block distress without further suppressing consciousness or breathing** beyond what is unavoidable.

#### 6. Continue holistic care (2 marks)

- **Pain control around the clock.**
- **Oral care, lip lubrication** (petroleum jelly, not glycerin).
- **Cluster activities**, dim lights, reduce noise.
- **Maintain skin integrity** — reposition every 2 hours.

#### 7. Support the family (2 marks)

- Explain what is happening in simple language — name what they are seeing.
- Reassure them: *"This is part of the dying process. He is not suffering as much as it looks. Our medicines will keep him comfortable."*
- Encourage gentle presence — touch, soft voice, soothing music.
- Allow them to step out if it is overwhelming; they are not abandoning him.
- Prepare them that this may be a sign that **death is near**.

## Case 14 — A Patient Asks "Am I Dying?" (20 marks)

> Ms. Yeboah, 45, has advanced pancreatic cancer. During a routine assessment she takes your hand and asks quietly: *"Nurse, am I dying?"* Her husband, sitting beside her, looks alarmed and shakes his head at you.
>
> **Walk through how you would respond, applying the BREAK NEWS framework.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Pause and create the space (2 marks)

- Do not respond reflexively. **Sit down at her level**, make eye contact, take her hand if culturally appropriate.
- Acknowledge: *"That is a really important question. Let's talk about it properly."*
- Politely signal to the husband that the conversation will be honest and respectful.

#### 2. Be prepared (B) (2 marks)

- Ensure you have the latest information about her condition.
- Have **enough time** — do not start when you only have a few minutes.
- **Prevent interruptions** — close the door, silence the phone, hand off other tasks if possible.

#### 3. Relatives present (R) (2 marks)

- Her husband is already with her — this is helpful.
- Briefly check: *"Would you like your husband to stay for this conversation?"* — honouring her autonomy.
- If she says yes, his presence supports her and they will share the same information.

#### 4. Expectations (E) (3 marks)

Find out what she already knows and what she is expecting to hear:

- *"What have the doctors told you about your illness so far?"*
- *"How do you think things are going?"*
- *"What made you ask that question today?"*

This is essential — she may have inferred from her body, family behaviour, or earlier conversations. What she has been told and what she has heard may be very different.

#### 5. Assess (A) what is appropriate (2 marks)

- *"Would you like me to tell you what I think is happening?"*
- Respect her response. If she says yes, proceed. If she says she is not ready, that is okay — you have not forced information on her.
- You can revisit next time.

#### 6. Knowledge sharing (K) — slowly and gently (3 marks)

- Give a **warning shot**: *"What I have to share is quite serious."*
- Use **plain language** — avoid medical jargon.
- Pause; allow her time to absorb.
- Example: *"The cancer has progressed despite the treatments we've tried. We are not able to cure it. The time we have with you is becoming shorter."*

#### 7. Never (N) say "there is nothing we can do" (2 marks)

Always pair what you cannot do with what you **can**:

- *"We cannot cure the cancer, but we have medicine that will control your pain."*
- *"We are always here to help if new problems come up."*
- *"We will keep you comfortable and support your family."*

#### 8. Empathize (E) (2 marks)

- Allow tears. Do not rush to stop them — crying is a normal, helpful reaction.
- Use empathic statements: *"This must be very difficult to hear."*
- Sit with her — silence is okay.
- Touch her hand if appropriate.

#### 9. Way forward (W) (2 marks)

- *"Let's talk about what happens next."*
- Outline the immediate care plan — pain control, symptom management, family support.
- **Fix a time** to meet again: *"I will come back tomorrow morning to see how you are and we can talk more then."*
- Reassure: *"We will not give up on you."*

#### 10. Stop and reflect (S) (0–1 mark — bonus)

After the conversation, **pause to reflect**. Breaking bad news is difficult for the nurse too. Take a moment before the next task to process what just happened and how you are feeling. If 20 marks total includes this, count it; otherwise this is the closing reminder.

## Case 15 — Family Wants You to Withhold the Diagnosis (15 marks)

> Mr. Asare's adult children pull you aside before you enter their father's room. They say: *"Please do not tell him he has cancer. He is an old man, he will lose hope, and it will kill him faster. Just say it is an infection."*
>
> **Counsel the family without breaking ethical principles.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Acknowledge their love and concern (2 marks)

Their request comes from love. Do not dismiss it. Sit with them, listen, and acknowledge: *"You clearly love your father and want to protect him. Let's talk about this together."*

#### 2. Explain why truth-telling matters (4 marks)

- **Maintains trust** — lying destroys the relationship between the patient and the care team.
- **Reduces uncertainty** — people cope better with truth than with uncertainty, even if the truth is painful.
- **Prevents unrealistic hope** — patients spend time and money seeking treatments because no one told them the truth.
- **Allows preparation** — writing a will, settling debts, mending difficult relationships, exploring spiritual issues, travelling to the family home, planning a funeral.
- **Studies show** patients usually want to know and cope better when they do.

#### 3. Address the "it will kill him" fear (3 marks)

- The reverse is often true — patients who are kept in the dark sense that something is wrong, suffer in silence, and **lose trust in the team**.
- Most patients **already suspect** something serious. Confirming reality is often a **relief**, not a shock.
- False reassurance ("Don't worry, you will be fine") leaves him alone with his doubts and fears.

#### 4. Offer the family a path forward (3 marks)

- Reassure them you will not simply blurt out the diagnosis at the door.
- Use the **BREAK NEWS** framework — at his pace, with their support, gentle warning shots, and never "nothing we can do."
- Invite them to be present during the conversation.
- They can choose how much to say in the first conversation; more can be shared later.

#### 5. State your limit clearly (2 marks)

You can say: *"I will not lie to your father. If he asks me directly whether he has cancer, I will not deny it. But I will not force information on him either."* This honours both your professional ethics (veracity, autonomy) and their concern.

#### 6. Recognise cultural context (1 mark)

In many cultures, family-mediated disclosure is the norm. Respect that — but never lie. The goal is to find a way that honours both **truth** and **culture**.

## Case 16 — Sudden Death Bereavement Support (20 marks)

> Mrs. Boateng's 17-year-old son was killed in a road traffic accident yesterday. She is in the family room, alternating between silence and sobbing. She says: *"This is not real. He was supposed to take his exams next week."*
>
> **Describe how you would support her, applying Worden's tasks of mourning and grief support principles.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Recognise the nature of sudden grief (2 marks)

Sudden, traumatic loss often produces **more intense and prolonged grief** than expected death. There has been no anticipatory grief, no chance to say goodbye. Be especially patient and present.

#### 2. Reach out with presence (2 marks)

- Approach gently. Sit at her level. Do not force conversation.
- Express sympathy simply: *"I am so sorry for your loss."*
- Allow silence — your **presence is enough**.

#### 3. Worden Task 1 — Accept the reality of the loss (3 marks)

- Her statement *"this is not real"* reflects shock and disbelief — normal early manifestations.
- Help gently confront reality through **clear language** ("died," "was killed") — avoid euphemisms that confuse.
- Support participation in traditional rituals — viewing the body, funeral, memorial service — these help confront the finality.

#### 4. Worden Task 2 — Experience the pain of grief (4 marks)

- Allow her to express affective symptoms — sadness, anger, guilt ("if only I had…"), shock, numbness.
- Recognise physical manifestations — chest tightness, breathlessness, fatigue, dry mouth.
- Recognise cognitive — disbelief, preoccupation, even hallucinations of her son.
- **Do not stop her crying** — it is a normal and helpful reaction.
- Empathize: *"This must be unbearable. There is nothing I can say that will make it less painful."*

#### 5. Worden Task 3 — Adjust to a new environment (3 marks)

- This is not for today — Task 3 typically begins **several months** later.
- Help her think about **practical immediate needs** — who is with her, who is collecting other children, who is making funeral arrangements.
- In the coming weeks/months, help her develop new daily routines.
- Connect her with **community resources** and **support groups**.

#### 6. Worden Task 4 — Reinvest energy (2 marks)

- Far too early to address directly today.
- In the long term, support her in finding meaningful activities — possibly memorial activities (charity, advocacy) — that honour her son.
- Reassure that this **does not mean forgetting him**.

#### 7. Reassure she is not to blame (2 marks)

- Sudden death often produces intense guilt: *"I should have driven him myself", "If only I had not let him go."*
- Reassure that **she did everything possible** and is not responsible.

#### 8. Educate about normal grief (1 mark)

- Tell her that what she is feeling is **normal**.
- **Upsurges** on birthdays, anniversaries, and unexpected reminders are also normal.
- Manifestations of grief vary widely from person to person.

#### 9. Watch for dysfunctional grief (1 mark)

Over time, monitor for:
- Denial lasting >6 months
- Severe physiological symptoms (choking, uncontrolled trembling)
- Suicidal thoughts
- Self-neglect

If these emerge, **refer for specialist mental health support**.

## Case 17 — Recognising Dysfunctional (Prolonged) Grief (15 marks)

> Nine months after her husband's death, Mrs. Owusu has stopped eating regularly, refuses to speak about him, keeps his clothes laid out, and tells you, *"He will come back. I know he will."* Her daughter says she has lost weight and barely leaves the house.
>
> **Identify the type of grief and describe your nursing actions.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Identify the diagnosis (3 marks)

This is consistent with **prolonged grief disorder** (formerly "complicated grief"):

- **Denial of the loss longer than 6 months** — *"He will come back."*
- **Self-neglect** — not eating, weight loss, social withdrawal.
- **Inability to move forward** — keeping clothes laid out as if he is returning.
- Recurrent intrusive thoughts and severe distress.

#### 2. Distinguish from normal grief (3 marks)

- Normal grief is **dynamic** — sad emotions ebb and flow, but the bereaved gradually accepts reality and resumes function.
- Mrs. Owusu is **"stuck"** — Worden's Task 1 (accept the reality) has not been accomplished, blocking the other tasks.
- Manifestations beyond normal: severe physiological symptoms, withdrawal from life, inability to acknowledge the death.
- Watch for **suicidal thoughts** — a hallmark of dysfunctional grief that requires immediate action.

#### 3. Reach out and listen (2 marks)

- Visit her or invite her in.
- Express sympathy and concern: *"I have been thinking about you. How are you really doing?"*
- Listen **non-judgmentally**. Do not contradict her belief that he is returning — meet her where she is.

#### 4. Gently address the loss (3 marks)

- Over time and with rapport, help her gently **acknowledge the death** — using clear words, supporting her through funeral pictures or rituals.
- Re-engage **traditional rituals** if not completed (memorial services, anniversary observances) — these help confront finality.
- Encourage her to **share memories** of her husband — not to forget, but to integrate the loss.

#### 5. Refer for specialist support (2 marks)

- **Mental health referral** is appropriate here — counselling or psychotherapy for prolonged grief.
- If suicidal thoughts emerge, refer **urgently**.
- Consider involving family members and community/religious support.

#### 6. Address physical and social neglect (2 marks)

- Address eating and weight loss — small frequent meals; family-supported routine.
- Re-establish daily structure — short walks, simple activities.
- Involve **social worker** for practical issues (finances, isolation).

## Case 18 — Paediatric Palliative Care (20 marks)

> Kojo, 7, has end-stage leukaemia. He is in pain but reluctant to say so. His mother is at his bedside, distressed and trying to feed him snacks he refuses. The team is reluctant to start morphine in a child.
>
> **Describe how you would assess and manage Kojo's pain holistically.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Core principle: children are not small adults (2 marks)

- Children have their own needs, opinions, and capabilities.
- They often **understand more than adults realise**.
- Honest communication and child-centred care are the foundation.

#### 2. Pain assessment in children (4 marks)

Direct self-report can be difficult. Use:

- **Observation** — watch him while you talk to his mother, or while he plays. Behaviour reveals pain when words don't.
- **Signs of pain in children** — crying, distressed face, not wanting to move or eat, poor concentration, irritability, sleep difficulty, increased breathing or heart rate.
- **Self-report tools when possible** — Wong-Baker FACES scale (usable from age 3) or pointing to faces showing degrees of distress.
- **Number of fingers** — older children can hold up fingers to show severity.
- **Listen to the child, listen to the carer, observe with your eyes.**

#### 3. Pain management — same WHO principles (4 marks)

The principles are identical to adults:

- **By the mouth** — oral preferred.
- **By the clock** — regular dosing.
- **By the ladder** — start with non-opioids, escalate as needed.
- **By the individual** — titrate.

**Special considerations:**

- **Avoid aspirin** in children — risk of **Reye's syndrome** (potentially fatal liver disease).
- **Paracetamol, ibuprofen, diclofenac** are safe and helpful.
- **Avoid intramuscular injections** — particularly distressing for children. **Rectal** route is useful for some drugs.
- Opioids (including morphine) **can be used in children** when needed, in appropriate weight-based doses, with monitoring. Refusing morphine to a child in severe pain is harmful.

#### 4. Non-pharmacological measures (4 marks)

Many tools the team and family can use without medicine — show them *"there is never nothing they can do"*:

- **Make him feel secure** — do not leave him alone if afraid.
- **Keep him in familiar surroundings** — home if possible; familiar toys and food in hospital.
- **Touch** — cuddling, carrying, gentle massage.
- **Play** — distraction is good medicine for children.
- **Music and stories.**
- **Make him feel valued** — listen to him, show his needs matter.

#### 5. Communicate with Kojo directly (3 marks)

- **Address him directly**, not just his mother.
- **Get down to his level** — sit beside him on the floor.
- **Avoid direct eye contact** if it feels threatening — talk while playing or drawing.
- Use **age-appropriate language**.
- Make sure he knows it is **okay to ask questions** and to say what he feels.
- Never lie — it destroys trust.

#### 6. Support the mother (3 marks)

- Acknowledge her distress.
- Recognise that **never force-feeding** is correct — *"a snack will often help, but never force."*
- Show her she is not powerless — teach her the non-drug comfort measures.
- Counsel her on truth-telling with Kojo: hearing the truth, age-appropriately, can be **a relief** for children — it may not be as bad as their secret fears.
- Prepare her for the dying process.

## Case 19 — Culturally Appropriate Palliative Care (20 marks)

> A traditional family in a rural Ghanaian community refuses to allow their dying matriarch, Nana Ama, to receive morphine. They say painkillers will *"interfere with the ancestors' calling her home."* They want only herbal preparations from the local healer. The team is frustrated.
>
> **Describe how you would provide culturally appropriate palliative care.**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Start with cultural awareness — examine your own bias (2 marks)

- Acknowledge your own beliefs and how they shape your reaction to the family's wishes.
- **Avoid ethnocentric and stereotyped attitudes** — research shows these cause real practice deficits.
- Recognise that the family's beliefs are **valid within their cultural framework**, not "wrong."

#### 2. Build cultural knowledge (3 marks)

- Learn about the **specific community's beliefs** about death, dying, ancestors, and traditional medicine.
- Patients and families are the **best source** of information about their culture — ask, listen.
- Do not assume — degree of acculturation varies among individuals within any culture.
- Read about predominant cultural groups in your area; use cultural pocket guides if available.

#### 3. Cultural skill — perform a cultural assessment (4 marks)

Explore systematically:

- **Lifestyle, values, norms, and expressions** of the family.
- **Taboos, myths, and misconceptions** about morphine and pain.
- **Folk and traditional healing systems** in use.
- **Patterns of health and illness** in this community.
- **Language** preferences.
- **Structure of service** — who in the family makes decisions; who provides care.
- **Patient's** views — does Nana Ama herself want the morphine or not? Her autonomy matters.

#### 4. Engage respectfully with the family (3 marks)

- **Do not impose** the WHO ladder on a family who fears it.
- Approach the **traditional healer with respect** — propose collaboration, not replacement.
- Acknowledge that herbal preparations have meaning to the family even if pharmacologically limited.
- Frame morphine differently if possible: *"This medicine does not interfere with her spirit — it only takes away her pain so she can be at peace with her family."*

#### 5. Support cultural rituals (2 marks)

- **Support the family to perform and complete cultural rituals** — being present with the dying, blessings, ancestral prayers.
- Provide privacy for these rituals where possible.
- Engage local elders, chaplains, or respected community figures who can communicate within the family's framework.

#### 6. Negotiate a culturally appropriate care plan (3 marks)

- **Offer**, do not impose: explain what morphine will do for her physically; respect their decision.
- **Find common ground** — can paracetamol be acceptable? Can morphine be reframed as "comfort medicine"?
- Address other domains they may welcome: oral care, positioning, gentle hygiene, music, spiritual presence.
- Continue to be present and supportive even if the family declines some interventions.

#### 7. Accept your own limitations (2 marks)

- Not every family will accept everything the team offers.
- Your role is to **provide compassionate, culturally sensitive care** within the family's framework — not to override their values.
- Document the family's wishes and continue offering support; revisit gently as trust grows.

#### 8. Use appropriate language (1 mark)

- Use the **family's language and terminology**.
- Avoid medical jargon. If interpreters are needed, use them well.

## Case 20 — Recognising and Managing Caregiver Stress (15 marks)

> You are a charge nurse on a palliative care unit. One of your colleagues, Esi, has been working extra shifts. You notice she is irritable, has lost weight, and snapped at a family yesterday — uncharacteristic of her. She tells you, *"I just can't sleep. Every patient I've lost this month is in my head."*
>
> **How do you recognise and address her stress, applying caregiver stress management principles?**

### Model Answer

#### 1. Recognise the signs of caregiver stress (4 marks)

**Physical signs** Esi is showing:

- **Sleep problems** ("can't sleep")
- **Fatigue** and weight change
- Possibly increased heart rate, headaches, muscle tension

**Psychosocial signs:**

- **Irritability** and **snapping** at families
- **Confusion / forgetfulness** possible
- Possible withdrawal, complaints of anxiety

The pattern fits Selye's definition — a **non-specific response to a demand for change** that has become overwhelming.

#### 2. Approach with empathy (2 marks)

- Approach her privately, without judgement.
- *"I have noticed you've been working very hard and seem worn out. How are you, really?"*
- Listen — do not lecture.

#### 3. Apply self-care strategies (4 marks)

Help her develop a personal stress-management plan:

- **Develop a self-care philosophy** — be attentive to her own needs.
- **Get to know herself** — clarify values; notice her reactions and stressors.
- **Avoid being self-critical** — use cognitive restructuring, self-compassion, mindfulness, positive affirmations.
- **Set time aside** each day to plan and reflect.
- **Develop social support** — colleagues, friends, family.
- **Communicate clearly and non-aggressively.**
- **Nutrition, exercise, sufficient sleep** — fundamentals.
- **Relax** — deep breathing, progressive relaxation, visualisation.
- **Manage time** effectively.
- **Spiritually become aware** — spirituality gives strength to face moral and ethical dilemmas.
- **Cultivate humour.**
- **Avoid addictive substances**.

#### 4. Address workload (2 marks)

- Review her shift load with the unit manager — reduce extra shifts if possible.
- Encourage her to **take time off** and use her leave.
- Consider rotation through less acute areas.

#### 5. Build team resilience (2 marks)

- The challenge of caring for people with different needs is stressful — this is **normal**.
- Combining family life and work is hard; shift work strains health.
- *"Let's take time off to make our work more enjoyable."*
- Create regular team debriefs, especially after patient deaths.
- Recognise the contribution of all team members.

#### 6. Refer if needed (1 mark)

If her symptoms persist or worsen — sleeplessness, depression, suicidal ideation — refer for **professional mental-health support**. Burnout is a clinical condition.

## How to Use These Model Answers Effectively

1. **Attempt the question first** before reading the model. Write or speak through your answer with a timer (target ~1.5 min per mark — so a 30-mark question deserves ~45 minutes).
2. **Tick the points** in the model that you covered. Be honest — partial coverage = partial marks.
3. **Identify your gaps.** Each section links back to the relevant topic file in this folder (e.g., [Pain Management & WHO Ladder](13-pain-management-who-ladder.md), [Communication](19-communication.md), [Bereavement & Grief](20-bereavement-grief-mourning.md)).
4. **Re-attempt after a few days** without re-reading the model — see if your gaps have closed.
5. **For case studies**, practice writing under exam conditions. Real exam answers must be **structured** (headings, numbered points), **specific** (drug names, doses, frameworks), and **comprehensive** (cover all marked sections).
