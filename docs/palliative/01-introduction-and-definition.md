# Introduction & Definition of Palliative Care

## Course Overview

This course provides students the opportunity to acquire knowledge and skill in supportive intervention for patients and families living with life-threatening illness. Students are introduced to:

- The concept of palliative care
- Caring for patients with life-threatening illnesses
- Managing the patient and family at the end stage of illness

Students are expected to identify and care for a patient/family with life-threatening illness as part of the course.

## Course Objectives

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

1. Define palliative care
2. Apply principles and ethics of palliative care in practice
3. Identify the models of palliative care
4. Recognize patients who need palliative care
5. Assess and manage pain and other symptoms effectively
6. Communicate well with dying persons and their families
7. Describe the process of grief and bereavement

## Definition of Palliative Care

**Palliative care** is an active holistic care of patients whose disease is not responsive to curative treatment. It emphasizes control of pain and other distressing physical, psychosocial, and spiritual concerns of those with life-limiting illness (WHO, 2005).

Palliative care aims to **prevent and relieve suffering** by early identification, assessment, and treatment of pain and other types of physical, psychological, emotional, and spiritual distress.

It focuses on providing patients with relief from the symptoms, pain, and stress of a serious illness whatever the diagnosis. The goal is to improve quality of life for both the patient and the family.

Palliative care strives to help individuals and their families address:

- Physical issues
- Psychological issues
- Social issues
- Spiritual issues
- Practical issues
- Associated expectations, needs, hopes, and fears

It also helps individuals and their families:

- Prepare for and manage life closure and the dying process
- Cope with loss and grief during the illness and bereavement

Palliative care approaches can coexist with curative interventions such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and include investigations needed to better understand and manage distressing clinical complications.

## Philosophy

Palliative care is a **philosophy** that provides a compassionate and supportive approach to clients and families who are living with life-threatening illnesses. This holistic approach **neither hastens nor postpones death** but provides relief of symptoms experienced by the dying client (Matzo & Sherman, 2001b).

The philosophy of palliative care is **holistic and client-centered**.

## The Interdisciplinary Team

Palliative care is planned and delivered through the collaborative effort of an interdisciplinary team beginning at the time the illness is diagnosed and continuing through the bereavement period.

The team's focus is on:

- Relieving suffering
- Controlling symptoms
- Supporting the patient's functional capacity

**Members of the team include:**

- Patients
- Families
- Physicians
- Nurses
- Pharmacists
- Social workers
- Chaplains
- Occupational therapists
- Speech therapists
- Respiratory therapists
- And many more

## Why the Need for Palliative Care?

- In 2020, approximately **10 million people died from cancer** worldwide (WHO, 2023).
- In 2022, around **630,000 people died from AIDS-related illnesses** globally (UNAIDS, 2023).
- Over **71% of people with advanced cancer or AIDS** experience moderate to severe pain and other distressing symptoms (WHO, 2023).
- As of 2023, about **39 million people are living with HIV** worldwide (UNAIDS, 2023).
- More than **56.8 million people globally require palliative care each year**, but only **14% receive it** (WHO, 2023).

## What Is Different About Palliative Care?

Health workers tend to focus on physical problems — diseases and treatments. But palliative care recognises that people are much more than just bodies — our minds, our spirits, and our emotions are part of who we are, as are the families and communities to which we belong.

So the problems facing a sick person and their family are not just physical: there may be **psychological, social, and spiritual issues** which are just as important as the illness itself.

Sometimes problems in one area may worsen others — for example, pain is often worse when people are anxious or depressed. It is only when we address all these areas that we are helping the whole person. This is called **holistic care**.

> **Illustrative case:** Imagine a young woman with three children living in a shanty town. Her husband died six months ago and neighbours say he must have had HIV. Now she is becoming sick, has lost weight, and is scared she may also die. She developed a painful, ulcerating, smelling sore on her leg which stops her sleeping. Some days she can barely get out of bed to care for her children, but her parents are far away. The landlord is asking for rent but she has no income. The neighbours are gossiping that the family is cursed, and she wonders if they are right, since she has prayed for help but none has come.
>
> If you were that woman, what would be on your mind? Her illness is only one of her many problems. Her greatest worry might be how to put food on the table, or what will happen to her children if she dies. She has no financial support; she is isolated and feels rejected by God.

**Palliative care is about people rather than diseases**, and seeks to address the problems which are of most concern to the patient.

## Conditions That May Require Palliative Care

- Cancer
- Heart disease
- Lung diseases (including COPD)
- Kidney failure
- Liver disease
- HIV / AIDS
- Neurological disorders (Parkinson's disease, dementia)
- Stroke
