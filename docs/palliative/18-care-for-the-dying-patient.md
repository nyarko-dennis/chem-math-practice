# Care for the Dying Patient

The goals of care for the dying patient change from **curative to palliative**: helping the patient live comfortably, and supporting them and their family emotionally and spiritually.

This document outlines the **specific nursing care priorities** for the dying patient.

## 1. Assessing Needs

Nurses initially focus assessment on the client's **basic physical needs**, such as:

- Pain
- Breathing
- Nutrition
- Hydration
- Elimination

They then include the **psychosocial and spiritual needs** of the client and family.

> Try not to repeat unnecessary assessments — allow the client to rest. Make **frequent checks without being physically intrusive**. This frequency provides security so the client does not feel abandoned.

## 2. Controlling Pain

**Primary objective:** Block pain **without suppressing level of consciousness or breathing**.

- Give pain medications on a **routine schedule around the clock** to avoid intense discomfort followed by heavy sedation.
- Regular dosing sustains a **plateau of continuous pain relief** and prevents exhausting the client.
- **Reassure** that frequent narcotic analgesia will not cause addiction.
- Physician may prescribe **mild tranquilizers or antidepressants** to reduce fear and anxiety, since fear and anxiety can exacerbate pain.

See [Pain Management & WHO Ladder](13-pain-management-who-ladder.md) for full guidance.

## 3. Facilitating Breathing

- Place the client in a **Fowler's position** to ease difficulty with breathing.
- If the client cannot cough and raise secretions, **gently suction**.
- Suctioning will **not** clear the lungs or ease breathing if the client has **pulmonary edema** (fluid in the lungs from heart failure). In this case, the physician may prescribe a sedative to relieve the anxiety created by the feeling of suffocation.
- **Oxygen** eventually may be used.

## 4. Administering Food and Fluids

If the client can take oral fluids and food:

- Offer **nourishment frequently in small amounts**
- Serve at the **appropriate temperature**
- Encourage the family to bring foods the client **likes** or that are traditional in the family's diet

### Cautions

- **Difficulty in swallowing, gastric and intestinal distention, and vomiting** create a potential for aspiration of fluids, as well as a decrease in food intake.
- **Administer antiemetics** about 1 hour before meals.
- **Do not insist** that the client eat or drink if symptoms cannot be controlled.
- **Report weight loss and inadequate intake** so the team can consider alternative nutritional and fluid administration routes.

## 5. Regulating Temperature

Skin temperature drops as death nears — the client may describe feeling cold.

- Provide **cotton socks, light blankets, and other light clothing** if the client feels chilled.
- **Gentle massage** of the arms and legs helps because it:
  - Transfers body heat from the hands to the skin surface
  - Improves circulation
  - Provides **support and communicates personal concern**

## 6. Maintaining Skin and Tissue Integrity

A drop in blood pressure and rapid heart failure lead to **poor tissue and organ perfusion**.

- **Reposition the client at least every 2 hours**.
- Poor tissue perfusion may cause **inadequate drug absorption** of intramuscular medications — consult with the physician about alternative routes.
- Drugs may be ordered to raise blood pressure, improve perfusion, and correct cardiac/circulatory problems.

## 7. Assisting with Self-Care and Activity

Dying clients may not tolerate physical activity well.

- Assist with **personal hygiene**.
- Keep the client **clean, well-groomed, and free of unpleasant odors** to promote dignity and self-esteem.
- Provide **oral care and ice chips**, because mouth breathing makes oral mucous membranes and lips dry.
- Use **petroleum jelly** to keep the lips lubricated.
- **Avoid glycerin applications** — they pull fluid from tissue and eventually worsen drying.

## 8. Promoting Sleep

A disturbance in sleep pattern may occur because of anxiety, fear, pain, or environmental stimuli such as bright lights and disturbing noise.

- **Cluster necessary activities** to avoid repeated awakenings.
- Protect the client from a steady stream of healthcare workers or visitors.
- **Dim lights** at night when possible.
- **Keep noise to a minimum**.
- The radio, television, or recordings of the client's **favorite music** may mask the continuous hum of equipment and monitors.

## 9. Facilitating Elimination

- Offer a bedpan or assist the client to the bathroom or bedside commode.
- **Incontinence** may occur because of disease process or proximity to death.
- Use **absorbent pads** when the client has lost bowel/bladder control.
- Assist with **thorough cleaning** to preserve skin integrity and dignity.
- The client may need an **indwelling or external catheter**, particularly if skin breakdown is a concern.

## 10. Addressing Fear, Social Isolation, Hopelessness, and Powerlessness

Because the dying client tends to become **isolated from others**, the nurse must spend time with them **apart from physical care**.

- **Be flexible** — interrupt physical care if the client indicates a need for companionship, support, and communication.
- During unplanned, spontaneous moments, clients often discuss **fears or concerns** — do not ignore or rush these.

### Communication posture

- **Sit down** next to the patient.
- **Lean forward** in the client's direction.
- Make **direct eye contact**.
- Use **nodding** and brief responsive comments (*"Yes," "I see"*).
- Allow **brief periods of silence** to encourage continued verbalization.

## 11. Addressing Spiritual Distress

- Religious beliefs and cultural customs influence attitudes about death.
- Clients may find great comfort from their religious faith and may want **someone associated with their religion** to visit.
- If the client indicates such a desire, **notify appropriate clergy**.
- If asked, the nurse may **pray with clients** or assist as indicated.
- When clients are too ill to express their wishes, **ask the family about spiritual care**.

## 12. Promoting Family Coping

People often find it difficult to communicate frankly with a dying person. **Failure to verbalize feelings** is often a source of regret for grieving relatives.

- Encourage family members to express feelings.
- Be a **compassionate listener**.
- If nurses encourage and listen to family members, family members may feel **more prepared to carry on a similarly honest dialogue** with the dying client.
- **Both relatives and the client** often experience comfort in this meaningful relationship.

### Practical support for family

- Provide a **private room** where the family can talk, cry, and rest.
- **Sit with the family** for a short time to offer emotional support.
- **Express concern for their welfare** and listen to their concerns.
- **Explain the measures** taken to provide comfort and pain relief.
- Prepare them: as death draws near, the dying client may appear to become **detached and unaware**, and may **slip into unconsciousness before death**.

## Summary Checklist

| # | Care Area | Key Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Assess needs | Physical first, then psychosocial/spiritual; do not over-disturb |
| 2 | Control pain | Around-the-clock dosing; preserve consciousness |
| 3 | Breathing | Fowler's position; suction gently; oxygen as needed |
| 4 | Food & fluids | Small frequent amounts; antiemetics 1 h before meals |
| 5 | Temperature | Warm coverings; gentle massage |
| 6 | Skin integrity | Reposition q.2h; consider non-IM routes |
| 7 | Self-care | Hygiene, oral care, lip lubrication (petroleum jelly, **not** glycerin) |
| 8 | Sleep | Cluster care; dim lights; reduce noise |
| 9 | Elimination | Bedpan/commode; absorbent pads; catheter if needed |
| 10 | Emotional support | Sit, lean in, eye contact, allow silence |
| 11 | Spiritual care | Honor faith; involve clergy as desired |
| 12 | Family support | Listen; provide space; prepare them for the dying process |
