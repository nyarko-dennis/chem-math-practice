# Bereavement, Grief & Worden's Tasks of Mourning

## Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Bereavement** | The **state of loss** — the experience of having suffered a loss; the subjective response experienced by surviving loved ones after the death of a person with whom they have shared a significant relationship |
| **Grief** | The **reaction to loss** — a normal dynamic process that occurs in response to any loss |
| **Mourning** | The way people **express grief** — usually related to cultural and religious values of the people |

> *"Grief is a sign that we loved something more than ourselves... [It] makes us worthy to suffer with the rest of the world."* — Joan Chittister

## The Nature of Grief

Grief is a **normal reaction to loss**. Grief occurs in response to:

- The **real loss** of a loved one
- The loss of **what might have been**

Grief is **dynamic** and includes both psychological and physiological responses. It is a powerful emotional state that **affects all aspects of a person's life** — it changes sleep, nutrition, and exercise habits, and triggers sad emotions.

Grief encompasses **physical, emotional, cognitive, spiritual, and social responses** to the loss. It is **highly individualized** and depends on the person's perception of the loss.

> Palliative care is also a holistic approach to medicine that **does not end with the death of a patient**. Caring for the bereaved family is a responsibility and a privilege.

## Manifestations of Grief (Worden, 2003)

### 1. Affective (Emotional) Symptoms
- Sadness
- Anger
- Guilt
- Anxiety
- Loneliness
- Helplessness
- Shock
- Numbness

### 2. Physical Symptoms
- Tightness in the chest and/or throat
- Breathlessness
- Oversensitivity to noise
- Muscle weakness
- Dry mouth
- Lack of energy

### 3. Cognitive Symptoms
- Disbelief
- Confusion
- Preoccupation
- Hallucinations

### 4. Behavioral Symptoms
- Sleep and appetite disturbance
- Social withdrawal
- Crying
- Visiting places as reminders
- Chronic anger
- Restlessness / over-activity

## Factors Influencing the Grief Process

6. Socioeconomic status
7. Cause of loss / death
8. Stigma
9. Personality traits and coping style

## Stages of Grief — Kübler-Ross Model

| Stage | What the Person May Say | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Denial** | *"No, not me. It cannot be true."* | Denies the loss has taken place and may withdraw. May last minutes to months. |
| **2. Anger** | *"Why me?"* | May be angry at the person who inflicted the hurt (even after death) or at the world for letting it happen. May also be angry with self for letting an event (e.g., a car accident) take place, even if nothing could have stopped it. |
| **3. Bargaining** | *"Yes me, but..."* | Makes bargains with God: *"If I do this, will you take away the loss?"* |
| **4. Depression** | *"Yes me, and I am sad."* | Feels numb, though anger and sadness may remain underneath. |
| **5. Acceptance** | *"Yes me, but it is okay."* | Anger, sadness, and mourning have tapered off. Accepts the reality of the loss. |

## Worden's Tasks of Mourning

J. W. Worden identifies **four tasks** in grieving:

1. **Accept the loss**
2. **Acknowledge the pain of the loss**
3. **Adjust to a new environment**
4. **Reinvest in the reality of a new life**

The tasks of grief are **not states of achievement** but a **fluctuating process** to accommodate a new normal lifestyle without what was lost. Accommodation implies an **active process** of adapting to fit specific circumstances, rather than a linear process with a final end point.

Some Sub-Saharan African palliative care givers and authors have adopted Worden's framework to illustrate the grief process and emphasize that **dealing with grief takes effort and need not follow a specific order**.

### Task 1 — Accept the Reality of the Loss

> *"Grief is love not wanting to let go."* — Earl A. Grollman

- Acknowledge and accept the reality that **the person is dead and will not return**.
- Help people accept the reality of the loss in their **own time and at their own pace**.
- **Traditional rituals** — funerals, memorial services — help people accomplish this task because they enable confronting the **finality of death**.

### Task 2 — Experience the Pain of Grief

> *"When tears come I am swimming in a hallowed stream. My heart is at work. My soul is awake."* — Mary Margaret Funk

- Help the bereaved express the pain in a **healthy way**.
- The pain of grief includes **physical, emotional, behavioural, cognitive, spiritual, and social responses** to the loss.
- This task is crucial: if the bereaved does not or cannot acknowledge the pain, these responses will **manifest in unhealthy ways** (e.g., somatization, substance use, dysfunctional grief).

### Task 3 — Adjust to the Loss

- Help the bereaved develop the **skills and fulfill roles** needed to move forward **without the physical presence of the deceased**.
- This can only begin **after several months** of dealing with the loss.
- May include adjusting to **living alone**, **looking for a job**, taking on new household roles, etc.

### Task 4 — Reinvest Energy from the Deceased into New Life

- Transfer the **emotional energy invested in the relationship with the deceased** into **new, healthy approaches to life**.
- Example: becoming active in **advocating for patients with breast cancer**.
- **This does not mean the deceased is forgotten.**

> **NB:** Those who grieve the death of a loved one **never truly get over it**. Even after they accomplish the tasks, the pain of the loss is still present — though **less intense and more manageable**.

## Dysfunctional Grief (Prolonged Grief Disorder)

**Prolonged grief disorder** (formerly called "complicated grief") describes **prolonged and intense mourning**.

### Symptoms of Prolonged Grief Disorder

- Recurrent and severe distressing emotions and intrusive thoughts related to the loss
- **Self-neglect**
- **Denial of the loss for longer than 6 months**
- Feeling **"stuck"** and unable to move forward

### Manifestations of Dysfunctional Grief

1. **Extended time of denial**
2. **Depression**
3. **Severe physiological symptoms** — choking, tightness in the chest, uncontrolled trembling
4. **Suicidal thoughts**

## Supporting / Helping People Who Have Lost Loved Ones

1. **Reach out** to the bereaved by visiting them and **express your sympathy**.
2. **Reassure** them that they did everything possible and did not cause their loved one's death.
3. **Educate** the bereaved about **normal responses to grief**.
4. **Assure** them that it is normal to experience **upsurges in grief** related to significant days or events (e.g., birthdays, anniversaries).
5. **Allow them to grieve** and **listen non-judgmentally**.
6. **Assess** the bereaved person or family's **physical or situational needs** and address them.
7. **Help the bereaved recognize opportunities for personal growth**.
8. Provide **culturally specific support**.
9. **Discuss with and encourage them to use the most convenient rituals** to symbolize transition, continuity, and healing.
