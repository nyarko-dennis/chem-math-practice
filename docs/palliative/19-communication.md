# Communication in Palliative Care

Talking with patients and families is a very important part of palliative care. The good news is that **we can do it even if we have no drugs, equipment, or special premises**. However, we do need some **basic skills** to communicate effectively.

Some people find communication easier than others, but these are skills which **we can all learn and practise**.

## Communication Skills — Listening

Listening to patients is **as important as talking**. It:

- Helps us get information
- **Makes people feel valued** when we listen to their stories
- Allows them to voice questions and worries — which can bring great relief and take away some of the **isolation and fear** they may be feeling

### Listening Skills Checklist

- **Find a quiet place** if possible, where you will not be interrupted
- **Sit at the patient's level** — get down to them if they are in bed (kneel or crouch beside the bed, or find a chair)
- **Pay attention** and **keep eye contact**
- Use **active listening** — nod, use small comments like *"I see," "Yes," "That must be hard for you"*
- **Allow silence** — do not interrupt; silence is okay even when uncomfortable
- **Clarify and summarise** what the patient has said

## Communication Skills — Talking

- **Always be respectful and polite** — this makes the patient feel valued
- **Avoid medical terms** the patient may not understand
- **Give information rather than advice** — so they can decide for themselves what to do
- Only give **accurate information** — it is all right to say *"I don't know"*
- **Check the patient has understood** — if you have given instructions, ask them to explain to you what they will do
- Ask if they have any further questions

## How Much Should We Say?

In many cultures, sickness is **not discussed openly**. But as palliative care develops around the world, things are changing.

People often think that telling a patient they have an incurable illness will only make them worse. **Families often want to protect their loved ones** from bad news and will ask the health worker not to say anything to them.

> Studies from different settings have shown that **people usually do want to know the truth** and **cope better if they do**.

We may need to spend time **counselling families** about this — explaining why it is important for the patient to know, and offering our help and support with difficult discussions.

It can be tempting to give false reassurance: *"Don't worry, you will be fine."* But the patient usually knows in their heart that they will not be fine, and is left to cope with doubts, fears, and questions on their own.

## Why It Is Good to Tell the Truth

- **To maintain a relationship of trust.** Lying destroys trust.
- **To reduce uncertainty.** People cope better with truth than with uncertainty, even if the truth is painful.
- **To prevent unrealistic hope.** Sometimes people spend much time and money going from clinic to clinic looking for treatment because no one has been brave enough to tell them there is no cure.
- **To give opportunity for mending difficult relationships** or exploring spiritual issues.
- **To allow patients and families to prepare for the future** — writing a will, travelling to the family home, planning a funeral, and many other things they want to do before they die.

## Breaking Bad News

> *"Truth is one of the most powerful medicines available to us, but we still need to develop a proper understanding of the right timing and dosage for each patient."* — Adapted from Simpson (1979)

Nobody finds this easy. Telling someone that they have cancer or HIV, or that there is no cure for their illness, can be traumatic. We may worry we won't know how to handle their reaction. **The good news is that it is a skill we can learn.**

There will be cultural differences in how it is done, but here are general guidelines using the **BREAK NEWS** framework.

### The "BREAK NEWS" Framework

#### B — Be Prepared
- Make sure you have **read or heard all the information** available about the patient.
- Make sure you have **enough time** — do not start when you only have a few minutes.
- Try to **prevent interruptions**; turn off your phone.

#### R — Relatives
- It is usually best to give bad news when the patient has a **close family member** with them for support and to share the information.
- Most people only remember a small part of what is said when they are anxious or upset.

#### E — Expectations
- Find out **what they already know**: *"What have you been told about your illness?"* / *"How do you think things are going?"*
- This is important **even if it seems obvious** — what has been said and what has been heard are not always the same.

#### A — Assess What Is Appropriate
- Try to assess **how much they would like to know**: *"Have you been wondering what this is about? Would you like me to tell you what I think is going on?"*
- It is not right to **force information** on someone not ready to hear it.
- It is also not right to **withhold information** from someone who does want to know.
- You do not have to say everything in one visit — they may be ready to hear more next time.

#### K — Knowledge Sharing
- Share the knowledge you have **slowly and gently**, being careful to avoid words they won't understand.
- Give a **warning shot** such as: *"We need to sit down and talk, things are quite serious."*
- Give them **time to absorb** what you are saying.
- At the end, **check they have understood**.

#### N — Never Say "There Is Nothing We Can Do"
- Give **positives as well as negatives**:
  - *"We cannot cure the cancer, but we have medicine that will stop the pain."*
  - *"We are always here to help if new problems arise."*

#### E — Empathize
- It can be hard to allow the patient to express feelings because we want to take away their sadness, or we are alarmed by their anger or despair.
- **We cannot remove their feelings, but we can empathize** — try to understand how they feel, putting yourself in their shoes.
- Say simple things like: *"This must be very difficult for you to hear."*
- It is **not helpful to stop a person from crying** — it is a normal and helpful reaction. Sit patiently — they will stop when they are ready.

#### W — Way Forward
- Talk about **what will happen next** and how you can help.
- **Fix a time** when you will see them again.
- Make sure they know **you will not give up on them** and that they know how to get help if problems arise before they see you next.

#### S — Stop and Reflect
- Breaking bad news is difficult for **us** as well as for the patient.
- It is good to **pause for a moment** before moving on to your next task — reflect on the conversation and how you are feeling.

## Summary

| Skill Area | Key Practices |
|---|---|
| **Listening** | Quiet space, sit at patient's level, eye contact, active listening, allow silence |
| **Talking** | Respectful, plain language, give information not advice, check understanding |
| **Truth-telling** | Builds trust, reduces uncertainty, allows preparation |
| **Breaking bad news (BREAK NEWS)** | Be prepared, Relatives present, Expectations, Assess appropriateness, Knowledge sharing, Never "nothing we can do," Empathize, Way forward, Stop and reflect |
