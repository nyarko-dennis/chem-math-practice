# Palliative Care in Children

> *"We cannot protect children from sadness, but we can support them through it."*

Sick children are among the **most vulnerable people in the world**. They are often unable to state their needs, may have their opinions disregarded or not sought, and yet they usually **understand far more about their situation than we realise**.

While the principles of palliative care for children are the **same as for adults** — holistic, patient-centred care aiming at quality of life — children also have **special needs to consider**.

## Points to Consider When Caring for Children

- **Children are not small adults** — they have their own needs.
- **Children are people** — they have opinions to be heard and can make choices.
- Children usually **know much more than we realise** and are often more capable than we think.
- **Hearing the truth can be a relief** — it may not be as bad as their secret fears.
- **Talking allows them to express their fears, sadness, and ask questions.**
- **Knowing the truth enables children to make choices.**

## Talking with Children

In many cultures, children are supposed to be *"seen but not heard."* They are often not expected to be part of important discussions about sickness and death.

It can be very hard to know how much to say to children of different ages about their own illness or about someone else in the family who is sick.

We should remember that **children often know much more than we think**, because they:

- Sense **anxiety** in others
- **Overhear conversations**
- See the effects of sickness in other families

### Communication Skills for All (Including Children)

- **Be respectful and polite**. Address the child directly if old enough to understand — **not just** the parent.
- **Talk *with* the child** rather than *to* them or *about* them.
- **Get down to their level** — sit next to them on the floor or kneel beside them.
- **Do not interrupt** when they are speaking — be patient.
- **Avoid words** they will not understand (varies by age).
- Make sure they know **it is all right to ask questions**.
- **Never force too much information** on someone.
- **Never lie** — it destroys your relationship of trust.

### Communication Skills Especially for Children

- Important conversations are **best carried out with a parent or guardian present**, unless the child requests to be alone.
- **Negotiate with parents/guardians** about one-to-one conversations with the child.
- **Direct eye contact may feel threatening** — children often find it easier to talk while **playing or drawing**.
- Tell the family: *"I will not give information to the child without your permission, but I will not lie."*

### Dealing with Difficult Questions

Children may ask questions like: *"Will I get better?"* or *"Is my mother going to die?"*

These are hard to answer, especially if we are not sure what the parents have said or are willing to say.

**Reflecting the question back** can help in finding out what they know and what they are most anxious about:

- *"I wonder what's made you ask that question?"*
- *"Have you asked your mother/father/grandmother about that? What did they say?"*
- *"We need to talk about that. Tell me what you think first, then I will tell you what I think."*

We want children to feel free to ask questions, **even if we cannot answer them fully at the time**:

- *"That is an important question. I want to discuss it when I have talked with your mother."*

If parents are unwilling for the child to be given information, **counsel them** that openness is usually helpful for families — but ultimately, **pass the responsibility back to them**:

- *"That is an important question — have you talked to your parents about it?"*

## Pain in Children

Assessing pain in children can be difficult. They may be **too young to say how they feel**, or **fear and anxiety may prevent them from speaking**.

Parents and carers can **underestimate or overestimate pain**.

The most important skill is **simple but careful observation**. Even if children cannot tell us about their pain, **they show us by how they behave**.

### Signs That a Child Is in Pain

- **Crying** and **distressed facial expression** (may be confused with anxiety)
- Not wanting to **move**
- Not wanting to **eat**
- **Poor concentration**
- **Irritability** or **restlessness**
- **Difficulty sleeping**
- **Increased breathing rate** or **heart rate**

### How to Observe Pain

> Watch children while you talk to their mothers, or while they are playing — when they do not realise you are watching and are **not anxious about what you are going to do** to them.

- **Listen to the child.**
- **Listen to the carer.**
- **Observe with your eyes.**

## Managing Pain in Children

### Pain Assessment Tools for Children

- **Older children** may use a simple pain score — showing severity by the **number of fingers** they hold up, or pointing at **pictures of smiling, sad, or distressed faces** (Wong-Baker FACES scale).
- Children as young as **3 years** can use the FACES scale.

### Pharmacological Management

The principles are the **same as for adults**:

- **By the mouth**
- **By the clock**
- **By the ladder**

**Avoiding injections is particularly important with children.** The **rectal route** may be useful for some drugs.

#### Drug Choice in Children

- ⚠️ **Avoid aspirin in children** — it can cause a potentially fatal liver disease, **Reye's syndrome**.
- **Other NSAIDs** (e.g., ibuprofen, diclofenac) and **paracetamol** are very helpful.

### Non-Drug Measures to Reduce Pain in Children

As well as medication, there are many ways to reduce pain that we can use and teach families and carers. **There is never "nothing they can do"** to help their child:

- **Make them feel secure** — don't leave them alone if they are afraid.
- **Keep them in familiar surroundings** — care for them at home if possible, or encourage families to stay with them in hospital and bring familiar toys or food from home.
- **Use touch** — cuddling, carrying, breastfeeding, massage.
- **Make them feel valued** — listen to them; show them their needs are important.
- **Feeding** — never force-feed, but a snack often helps.
- **Play with them** — distraction is good medicine for children.
- **Use music and stories**.

## Summary

| Area | Key Principle |
|---|---|
| **Mindset** | Children are not small adults; they understand more than we think |
| **Communication** | Talk with, not at; get to their level; never lie |
| **Difficult questions** | Reflect back; involve parents; offer to follow up |
| **Pain assessment** | Observe behavior; use FACES scale for children ≥3 years |
| **Pain meds** | Avoid aspirin (Reye's); ibuprofen, paracetamol; oral/rectal routes; **no injections** |
| **Non-drug measures** | Security, familiar surroundings, touch, play, music, stories |
